Light–Matter Interaction

From minimal coupling to ultrafast atomic dynamics

Kumar Azad

M.Sc.-level learning notes · 10 November 2026

Abstract

Light–matter interaction provides a bridge between classical electromagnetic fields and quantum dynamics. These notes derive the Hamiltonian of an electron in an applied field, explain the electric-dipole and gauge approximations, establish transition amplitudes and selection rules, and introduce ultrashort pulses. Hydrogen serves as a worked example, with a final section outlining mathematically controlled numerical calculations.

Status. Educational notes based on established quantum theory and the cited literature. These are not a claim of new research results. We use a prescribed classical electromagnetic field acting on a nonrelativistic electron; a fully quantized electromagnetic field requires a different Hamiltonian.

1. The model and electromagnetic coupling

Consider an electron of mass \(m_e\) and charge \(q=-e\), bound to a fixed proton. Let \(\mathbf r\) and \(\mathbf p=-i\hbar\nabla\) denote its position and canonical momentum operators. Without the applied field, the hydrogen Hamiltonian is

\[ H_0=\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m_e} -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}. \]

For externally prescribed scalar and vector potentials \(\phi(\mathbf r,t)\) and \(\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t)\), minimal coupling gives

\[ H(t)=\frac{[\mathbf p-q\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t)]^2}{2m_e} +q\phi(\mathbf r,t)-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}. \]

The classical fields obey \(\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A\) and \(\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A\). For wavelengths much larger than the atomic size \(a_0\), the dipole approximation replaces \(\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t)\) by a spatially uniform \(\mathbf A(t)\). A useful scale condition is \(ka_0\ll1\), where \(k=2\pi/\lambda\).

2. Velocity and length gauges

In the spatially uniform field approximation, choose \(\phi=0\) and \(\mathbf A=\mathbf A(t)\). The minimal-coupling Hamiltonian is called the velocity-gauge Hamiltonian:

\[ H_{\mathrm V}(t)=\frac{[\mathbf p+e\mathbf A(t)]^2}{2m_e} -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r},\qquad \mathbf E(t)=-\dot{\mathbf A}(t). \]

With the unitary transformation \(\psi_{\mathrm L}=U(t)\psi_{\mathrm V}\), where \(U(t)=\exp[i e\,\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf A(t)/\hbar]\), the length-gauge Hamiltonian follows from \(H_{\mathrm L}=UH_{\mathrm V}U^\dagger+i\hbar\dot U U^\dagger\):

\[ \begin{aligned} H_{\mathrm L}(t) &=H_0+e\,\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf E(t)\\ &=H_0-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E(t),\qquad \mathbf d=q\mathbf r=-e\mathbf r. \end{aligned} \]

This is an exact unitary equivalence within the spatially uniform, prescribed-field model. It does not imply that arbitrarily truncated atomic bases will produce identical results in both gauges. For spatially varying fields, magnetic effects, relativistic processes, or a quantized cavity field, the model needs additional terms or degrees of freedom.

3. Transition amplitudes and spectral response

Let \(H_0|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle\). Expand a state in stationary eigenstates (including continuum states when necessary) as \(|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_n(t)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle\). The time-dependent Schrödinger equation becomes

\[ i\hbar\,\dot c_f(t)= \sum_n e^{i\omega_{fn}t} \langle f|V(t)|n\rangle c_n(t), \qquad \omega_{fn}=\frac{E_f-E_n}{\hbar}. \]

For an initially occupied state \(|i\rangle\), a weak applied field, and a distinct final state \(|f\rangle\), first-order time-dependent perturbation theory gives

\[ \begin{aligned} c_f^{(1)}(t)&=-\frac{i}{\hbar} \int_{t_i}^{t}dt'\, e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\langle f|V(t')|i\rangle\\ &=\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t_i}^{t}dt'\, e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\, \mathbf d_{fi}\cdot\mathbf E(t'), \quad \mathbf d_{fi}=\langle f|\mathbf d|i\rangle. \end{aligned} \]

For linearly polarized light with fixed unit polarization \(\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\), write \(\mathbf E(t)=\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon} E(t)\). The transition probability to first nonvanishing order is

\[ P_{i\to f}\simeq \frac{|\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\cdot\mathbf d_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2} \left|\int_{t_i}^{t}dt'\,E(t')e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\right|^2. \]

Thus the transition probes the spectral content of the field at the Bohr frequency \(\omega_{fi}\), multiplied by the dipole matrix element. This formula assumes the perturbative regime; strong-field ionization and multiphoton effects require more complete dynamics.

4. Hydrogen and electric-dipole selection rules

For hydrogenic bound states \(|n\ell m\rangle\), electric-dipole transitions couple opposite-parity orbital states. In the spin-independent, nonrelativistic model, the orbital selection rules are

\[ \Delta\ell=\pm1,\qquad \Delta m=0,\pm1;\qquad \Delta m=0\ \text{for a field polarized along }z. \]

As an explicit example, let \(|100\rangle\) denote the \(1s\) state and \(|210\rangle\) the \(2p,m=0\) state. Writing \(\psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\varphi)=R_{n\ell}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\), one finds

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle 210|z|100\rangle &=\left[\int_0^\infty r^3R_{21}(r)R_{10}(r)\,dr\right] \left[\int Y_{10}^{*}\cos\theta\,Y_{00}\,d\Omega\right]\\ &=\frac{768a_0}{243\sqrt6}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt3} =\frac{256a_0}{243\sqrt2}. \end{aligned} \]

The electronic transition dipole is \(\langle 210|d_z|100\rangle=-e\,256a_0/(243\sqrt2)\), assuming a fixed proton and the usual real hydrogen wavefunctions. The familiar nonrelativistic level formula \(E_n\simeq-13.6\,\mathrm{eV}/n^2\) gives an energy separation near \(10.2\,\mathrm{eV}\), corresponding to the Lyman-\(\alpha\) spectral region. For precision spectroscopy one must account for reduced mass, fine structure, and other corrections. See the NIST selection-rule reference.

5. Ultrashort and attosecond fields

An attosecond is \(10^{-18}\,\mathrm s\). Ultrafast light pulses can probe electronic motion on its intrinsic timescales. To construct a finite, localized pulse with a vanishing time integral of the electric field, define a Gaussian-envelope vector potential (rather than choosing an unrelated electric-field envelope). With \(u=t-t_0\),

\[ \mathbf A(t)=\hat{\mathbf z}\,A_0\, e^{-u^2/(2\sigma^2)}\sin(\omega_0u+\phi). \]

The corresponding field is the negative time derivative,

\[ \mathbf E(t)= \hat{\mathbf z}\,A_0e^{-u^2/(2\sigma^2)} \left[ \frac{u}{\sigma^2}\sin(\omega_0u+\phi) -\omega_0\cos(\omega_0u+\phi) \right]. \]

Because \(\mathbf A(t)\to0\) as \(t\to\pm\infty\), this idealized pulse satisfies \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbf E(t)\,dt=0\). The parameter \(\sigma\) controls the temporal envelope, while \(\phi\) is the carrier-envelope phase. A pulse duration alone does not specify the photon energy or guarantee an allowed transition: both its spectrum and the matrix elements matter.

For a near-resonant, weakly driven two-level system, the rotating-wave approximation leads to resonant Rabi oscillations for an approximately constant amplitude:

\[ P_e(t)=\sin^2\left(\frac{\Omega_R t}{2}\right), \qquad \Omega_R=\frac{|\mathbf d_{eg}\cdot\mathbf E_0|}{\hbar}. \]

This formula is not generally valid for intense, isolated subcycle pulses. When the rotating-wave or two-level assumptions fail, solve the full time-dependent Hamiltonian and include sufficiently many bound and continuum states as appropriate. Attosecond science is reviewed by Corkum and Krausz.

6. Numerical methods and mathematical checks

A straightforward finite-basis treatment evolves expansion coefficients through the matrix Schrödinger equation. In the length gauge, with \(H_{mn}^{(0)}=E_n\delta_{mn}\), the equations read

\[ i\hbar\,\frac{d\mathbf a(t)}{dt} =\left[\operatorname{diag}(E_n)- \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E(t)\right]\mathbf a(t), \]

where \(\mathbf d\) denotes the matrix of dipole elements in the chosen basis. A reliable calculation must specify its state space, basis cutoff, field units, time step, final time, and boundary treatment. Check norm conservation for a Hermitian closed-system Hamiltonian, convergence against a larger basis and smaller time step, and agreement between gauges in the converged regime.

For the full hydrogen problem, spatial-grid and partial-wave approaches can treat continuum channels more faithfully. Extract excitation probabilities, bound-state populations, dipole expectation values, and—only with an appropriately represented continuum—ionization observables.

Possible later extension. A hydrogen atom subject to structured optical fields or to confinement in a toroidal domain would require a precisely specified geometry, self-adjoint boundary conditions, and a justified coupling between the applied field and the confined electron. Such a problem is an exploratory research direction, not an established result of the equations above.

7. Exercises and references

Exercises. (i) Derive the length-gauge interaction from the stated unitary transformation; verify the sign of the electron charge. (ii) Evaluate the \(1s\rightarrow2p,m=0\) dipole integral starting from the radial hydrogen functions. (iii) Fourier-transform the pulse constructed from \(\mathbf A(t)\); identify how its bandwidth changes with \(\sigma\). (iv) Numerically compare a two-level prediction with a larger basis as the pulse shortens.

These notes adopt a nonrelativistic, prescribed classical field and the electric-dipole approximation in most derivations. Other regimes require appropriate refinements.
