Positive Geometry

How a region’s boundaries determine a differential form—and why that idea matters for scattering amplitudes.

Kumar Azad · Introductory study guide

01 · The geometric idea

Start with a region such as a closed interval or a triangle. Instead of asking only for its size, ask for a differential form that records its boundaries. A canonical form has simple logarithmic poles at those boundaries; taking a residue produces the canonical form of the boundary itself.

More precisely, a positive geometry is a pair (X, X≥0): a complex projective algebraic variety defined over the reals, together with a suitable oriented, closed semialgebraic region of its real points. The definition recursively requires a unique rational top-degree form with the prescribed boundary residues and no other poles. Not every region satisfies these conditions. See [1] for the technical hypotheses.

ResB Ω(X, X≥0) = Ω(B, B≥0)

Locally near a boundary t = 0, the form looks like (dt/t) ∧ ΩB plus terms regular there. This explains the word “logarithmic”: dt/t = d log t. Orientations determine the residue signs; a zero-dimensional oriented point has canonical form +1 or −1.

02 · Canonical forms in action

The interval

For the interval a ≤ x ≤ b, oriented from a to b, choose

Ω[a,b] = (b − a) dx / [(x − a)(b − x)]
= dx/(x − a) + dx/(b − x)
= d log[(x − a)/(b − x)].

The numerator fixes the normalization. At infinity the two terms cancel their apparent pole. This is a differential form with boundary singularities, not a probability density with a finite integral over the closed interval.

The standard triangle

Consider the region Δ = {(x,y): x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0, x + y ≤ 1}, with orientation dx ∧ dy. Its canonical form is

ΩΔ = (dx ∧ dy) / [xy(1 − x − y)].

Along x = 0, write ΩΔ = (dx/x) ∧ dy/[y(1 − x − y)]. Its residue is dy/[y(1 − y)], the canonical form of the unit interval. This is the boundary recursion in a concrete calculation.

For a polygon, triangulation gives another useful principle: add the canonical forms of consistently oriented triangles. Contributions on internal diagonals cancel, leaving only poles on the polygon’s actual boundary. [1]

03 · The positive Grassmannian

The real Grassmannian Gr(k,n) parametrizes k-dimensional linear subspaces of ℝn. Represent one by the row span of a full-rank k × n matrix C; invertible row operations do not change the subspace.

ΔI(C) = det(Ci₁, …, Ciₖ) > 0
for every I = {i₁ < ⋯ < iₖ}.

The positive Grassmannian Gr>0(k,n) consists of subspaces admitting a representative whose ordered maximal minors are all positive. Its closure is the nonnegative Grassmannian Gr≥0(k,n), where those minors may vanish. Positivity concerns these determinants, not positivity of every matrix entry. The choice of positive representative is preserved by row transformations of positive determinant.

For example, take C with rows (1,1,1) and (0,1,2). Its ordered 2 × 2 minors are Δ12 = 1, Δ13 = 2 and Δ23 = 1, so it represents a point of Gr>0(2,3). The dimension of Gr(k,n) is k(n − k).

Patterns of vanishing minors organize the nonnegative Grassmannian into positroid cells. Planar networks and plabic graphs provide powerful ways to describe and parametrize these cells. Postnikov’s paper is a foundational reference. [2]

04 · An application: the amplituhedron

The tree amplituhedron is an image of a nonnegative Grassmannian under a map determined by positive external data. With an n × (k + m) matrix Z whose ordered maximal minors are positive, the map is

Y = CZ ∈ Gr(k, k + m),
𝒜n,k,m(Z) = {rowspan(CZ) : C ∈ Gr≥0(k,n)}.

Here n ≥ k + m; C is a k × n representative, and the notation uses the conventional closure of the positive image. For m = 2 and k = 1, this gives a convex polygon in projective two-space, a useful elementary model. The physics application uses m = 4.

In planar 𝒩 = 4 supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory, amplituhedron canonical forms encode scattering-amplitude data: tree amplitudes and, in the loop generalization, loop integrands. Recovering the physical superamplitude involves the appropriate kinematic and supersymmetric translation. [3]

Scope matters. This construction belongs to a special quantum field theory and a planar limit. It is not a general formula for QCD scattering or all quantum field theories. Loop integrands also differ from amplitudes after loop integration.

05 · A route into the literature

  1. Build the prerequisites: determinants, differential forms, residues, and elementary projective geometry.
  2. Work through the examples: verify the interval residues and the triangle’s boundary form; then triangulate a square.
  3. Study positivity: compute small Grassmannian minors before exploring positroid cells and networks.
  4. Connect to physics: learn color ordering, planar amplitudes, and momentum twistors before the m = 4 amplituhedron.
