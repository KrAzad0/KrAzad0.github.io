Positive Geometry

Motivation, construction, and the path to the amplituhedron

Kumar Azad

Learning notes · Revised 9 November 2026

Abstract

Why should scattering amplitudes be related to geometry? These notes follow the questions that make the connection natural: why diagrammatic calculations hide simplicity, how positivity organizes on-shell data, and why boundary residues suggest a canonical differential form. Elementary interval and triangle calculations illustrate the mechanism before the Grassmannian and amplituhedron constructions are introduced.

Status. An educational synthesis of the cited literature, not an original research paper or an arXiv submission. The motivating questions below are a pedagogical reconstruction of the published ideas, not quotations or claims about the authors’ private thought processes.

1. The problem and the development of the idea

1.1 Why look beyond a sum of diagrams?

A scattering amplitude predicts the quantum-mechanical weight of a scattering process. Feynman diagrams give a systematic way to calculate it, but individual terms can contain complexity that disappears in the final answer. The question is therefore structural: can one describe the final object in variables that make its simplicity and symmetries visible from the beginning?

This is a motivation for the amplituhedron program in planar 𝒩 = 4 supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory. It does not make Feynman diagrams incorrect; it asks what alternative mathematical object can organize the same amplitude data. [3]

1.2 Replace off-shell bookkeeping with on-shell building blocks

One route studies diagrams built from on-shell processes. In the 2012 Grassmannian formulation, these diagrams correspond to cells of the positive Grassmannian, and their measures take logarithmic forms in suitable positive coordinates. Combinatorics, geometry, and amplitude calculations become different descriptions of related data. [4]

The next question is: if individual contributions look like pieces of a geometry, is there a single region whose canonical form combines them? This is a useful conceptual bridge to the amplituhedron, rather than a proof of its construction.

1.3 Define the region first

Arkani-Hamed and Trnka’s 2013 amplituhedron proposal defines a geometric image of positive Grassmannian data. Instead of beginning with individual Feynman diagrams, the construction begins with positivity and seeks the form associated with the resulting region. Different decompositions can represent the same underlying geometric object. [3]

1.4 Extract the general mathematical principle

In 2017, Arkani-Hamed, Bai, and Lam formulated positive geometries and canonical forms as mathematical objects in their own right. The organizing principle is recursive: the form has logarithmic singularities on boundaries, and each boundary residue is its own canonical form. The framework includes examples beyond amplituhedra. [1]

The historical sequence and the teaching sequence differ. We now start with an interval, where the boundary rule can be derived explicitly, and then return to the more elaborate constructions.

2. From boundaries to a definition

Start with a region such as a closed interval or a triangle. Instead of asking only for its size, ask for a differential form that records its boundaries. A canonical form has simple logarithmic poles at those boundaries; taking a residue produces the canonical form of the boundary itself.

More precisely, a positive geometry is a pair \((X,X_{\geq 0})\): a complex projective algebraic variety defined over the reals, together with a suitable oriented, closed semialgebraic region of its real points. The definition recursively requires a unique rational top-degree form with the prescribed boundary residues and no other poles. Not every region satisfies these conditions. See [1] for the technical hypotheses.

\[ \operatorname{Res}_{B}\Omega(X,X_{\geq 0}) =\Omega(B,B_{\geq 0}) \]

Locally near a boundary \(t=0\), the form looks like \(\frac{dt}{t}\wedge\Omega_B\) plus terms regular there. This explains the word “logarithmic”: \(\frac{dt}{t}=d\log t\). Orientations determine the residue signs; a zero-dimensional oriented point has canonical form \(+1\) or \(-1\).

3. Deriving the elementary examples

The interval

Suppose the region is the interval \(a\leq x\leq b\), with \(a

\[ \begin{aligned} \Omega_{[a,b]}(x) &=\frac{(b-a)\,dx}{(x-a)(b-x)}\\[3pt] &=\frac{dx}{x-a}+\frac{dx}{b-x}\\[3pt] &=d\log\!\left(\frac{x-a}{b-x}\right). \end{aligned} \]

The numerator fixes the normalization. At infinity the two terms cancel their apparent pole. This is a differential form with boundary singularities, not a probability density with a finite integral over the closed interval.

The standard triangle

Consider the region \(\Delta=\{(x,y):x\geq0,\ y\geq0,\ x+y\leq1\}\), with orientation \(dx\wedge dy\). To encode the three edges, try a denominator \(xy(1-x-y)\). Requiring the residue on \(x=0\) to be the unit interval form fixes the constant numerator to \(1\). The resulting canonical form is

\[ \Omega_{\Delta}=\frac{dx\wedge dy}{x\,y\,(1-x-y)} \]

Along \(x=0\), write \(\Omega_{\Delta}=\frac{dx}{x}\wedge\frac{dy}{y(1-x-y)}\). Its residue is \(\operatorname{Res}_{x=0}\Omega_{\Delta}=\frac{dy}{y(1-y)}\), the canonical form of the unit interval, with the induced boundary orientation. This is the boundary recursion in a concrete calculation.

For a polygon, triangulation gives another useful principle: add the canonical forms of consistently oriented triangles. Contributions on internal diagonals cancel, leaving only poles on the polygon’s actual boundary. [1]

4. Why the positive Grassmannian?

Why move from polygons to spaces of planes? A Grassmannian packages linear relations while removing dependence on a choice of basis. Positivity then selects a structured region whose boundaries can be tracked through minors. This makes it a natural setting for the on-shell constructions discussed above. [4]

The real Grassmannian \(\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)\) parametrizes \(k\)-dimensional linear subspaces of \(\mathbb R^n\). Represent one by the row span of a full-rank \(k\times n\) matrix \(C\); invertible row operations do not change the subspace.

\[ \Delta_I(C)=\det(C_{i_1},\ldots,C_{i_k})>0, \qquad I=\{i_1<\cdots

The positive Grassmannian \(\operatorname{Gr}_{>0}(k,n)\) consists of subspaces admitting a representative whose ordered maximal minors are all positive. Its closure is the nonnegative Grassmannian \(\operatorname{Gr}_{\geq0}(k,n)\), where those minors may vanish. Positivity concerns these determinants, not positivity of every matrix entry. The choice of positive representative is preserved by row transformations of positive determinant.

For example, the following matrix has positive ordered maximal minors:

\[ C=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad (\Delta_{12},\Delta_{13},\Delta_{23})=(1,2,1)>0. \]

Thus \([C]\in\operatorname{Gr}_{>0}(2,3)\). In general, \(\dim \operatorname{Gr}(k,n)=k(n-k)\).

The minors are not independent. In \(\operatorname{Gr}(2,4)\), for example, the Plücker coordinates satisfy

\[ \Delta_{12}\Delta_{34}-\Delta_{13}\Delta_{24} +\Delta_{14}\Delta_{23}=0. \]

This identity follows by expanding the \(2\times2\) minors of a \(2\times4\) representative; it is an algebraic constraint on the Grassmannian, not an additional positivity assumption. Patterns of vanishing minors organize the nonnegative Grassmannian into positroid cells. Planar networks and plabic graphs provide powerful ways to describe and parametrize these cells. Postnikov’s paper is a foundational reference. [2]

5. From positive data to the amplituhedron

The tree amplituhedron is an image of a nonnegative Grassmannian under a map determined by positive external data. With an \(n\times(k+m)\) matrix \(Z\) whose ordered maximal minors are positive, the map is

\[ \begin{gathered} Y=\operatorname{rowspan}(CZ)\in\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+m),\\ \mathcal A_{n,k,m}(Z)= \bigl\{\operatorname{rowspan}(CZ):[C]\in\operatorname{Gr}_{\geq0}(k,n)\bigr\} \end{gathered} \]

Here \(n\geq k+m\); \(C\) is a \(k\times n\) representative, and \(Z\) is an \(n\times(k+m)\) matrix with strictly positive ordered maximal minors. The formula uses the nonnegative Grassmannian, including its boundary. For \(m=2\) and \(k=1\), this gives a convex polygon in projective two-space, a useful elementary model. The physics application uses \(m=4\).

In planar \(\mathcal N=4\) supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory, amplituhedron canonical forms encode scattering-amplitude data: tree amplitudes and, in the loop generalization, loop integrands. Recovering the physical superamplitude involves the appropriate kinematic and supersymmetric translation. [3]

Scope matters. This construction belongs to a special quantum field theory and a planar limit. It is not a general formula for QCD scattering or all quantum field theories. Loop integrands also differ from amplitudes after loop integration.

6. Further study and references

Suggested exercise. Starting from \(\Omega_{\Delta}=\frac{dx\wedge dy}{xy(1-x-y)}\), compute the residue at \(y=0\). Explain why it is the negative of \(\frac{dx}{x(1-x)}\) in the ambient coordinate convention and how the induced boundary orientation resolves the sign. For additional practice, calculate every ordered \(2\times2\) minor of a general \(2\times4\) matrix and verify the Plücker identity.
  1. Build the prerequisites: determinants, differential forms, residues, and elementary projective geometry.
  2. Work through the examples: verify the interval residues and the triangle’s boundary form; then triangulate a square.
  3. Study positivity: compute small Grassmannian minors before exploring positroid cells and networks.
  4. Connect to physics: learn color ordering, planar amplitudes, and momentum twistors before the m = 4 amplituhedron.
