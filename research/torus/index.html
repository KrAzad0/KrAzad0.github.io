01 / Overview

Abstract

This project investigates a hydrogen-like quantum system confined to a toroidal region and explores how the nontrivial geometry of the domain affects bound-state structure and time-dependent response to an ultrashort laser pulse.

The work has developed through two related descriptions: (i) an idealized particle constrained to a toroidal surface, where differential geometry enters through the Laplace–Beltrami operator and a curvature-induced potential; and (ii) a three-dimensional toroidal volume with hard-wall boundaries and a Coulomb attraction centred near the torus. The latter provides a route toward sparse-matrix eigenvalue calculations and time propagation with a time-dependent electric field.

The computational record includes exploratory two- and three-dimensional discretizations, preliminary ground-state energy estimates, and an initial attosecond-pulse modelling plan. The current objective is to consolidate these models, make each numerical configuration reproducible, and distinguish verified calculations from future physical interpretation.

02 / Research question

Motivation and questions

Most textbook hydrogen problems use spherical symmetry. Toroidal confinement changes the topology and boundary geometry of the allowed region, breaks the full rotational symmetry of free space, and can introduce nontrivial couplings between spatial directions. An external ultrashort pulse then probes the confined system away from its stationary states.

Central question

How do toroidal geometry, Coulomb binding and hard-wall confinement jointly shape the stationary and time-dependent quantum behaviour of a hydrogenic electron?

  • How do the eigenenergies and probability densities depend on the major radius and tube radius?
  • How does a Coulomb centre positioned within the toroidal region affect localization and spectral structure?
  • What changes when the system is driven by a short, linearly or elliptically polarized electric-field pulse?
  • Which observables can distinguish geometric confinement effects from ordinary laser-driven dynamics?
03 / Mathematical setup

Toroidal geometry and physical model

Let \(R\) denote the major radius and \(a\) the tube radius, with \(R>a>0\). In Cartesian coordinates, the solid torus is represented by

\[\Omega=\left\{(x,y,z):\left(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}-R\right)^2+z^2<a^2\right\}.\]

Equivalently, with cylindrical radius \(\rho=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), the boundary is the zero level set of

\[B(\rho,z)=a^2-(\rho-R)^2-z^2,\qquad \Omega=\{B>0\}.\]

The earlier model places the Coulomb centre near the point \(\mathbf r_p=(R,0,0)\), on the central circular axis of the torus. In atomic units (\(\hbar=m_e=e=4\pi\epsilon_0=1\)), the field-free Hamiltonian is written as

\[H_0=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^2-\frac{Z}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_p|},\qquad \mathbf r\in\Omega,\]

with a hard-wall (Dirichlet) condition \(\psi|_{\partial\Omega}=0\). Here \(Z=1\) for a hydrogenic proton. This is a model definition; the precise Coulomb-centre placement and treatment of the singularity must be stated explicitly in any reproducible calculation.

Geometric parameters

Major radius \(R\): distance from the symmetry axis to the centre of the tube. Minor radius \(a\): radius of the tube cross-section. Their ratio \(R/a\) controls the shape of the torus.

04 / Surface approximation

Thin-torus constrained-particle model

Analytical model explored

A separate idealization constrains the particle to the toroidal surface. A standard parametrization is

\[\mathbf X(\theta,\phi)=\big((R+a\cos\theta)\cos\phi,\ (R+a\cos\theta)\sin\phi,\ a\sin\theta\big),\]

with \(0\leq\theta,\phi<2\pi\). The induced metric is \(ds^2=a^2d\theta^2+(R+a\cos\theta)^2d\phi^2\), and the surface area element is \(a(R+a\cos\theta)\,d\theta\,d\phi\).

The kinetic operator for a particle intrinsically moving on the surface is the Laplace–Beltrami operator

\[\Delta_{\!LB}=\frac{1}{a(R+a\cos\theta)}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\frac{R+a\cos\theta}{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{(R+a\cos\theta)^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}.\]

For a particle confined to a curved surface by a strong normal potential, thin-layer quantization also produces the da Costa geometric potential

\[V_{\mathrm{geom}}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(M^2-K\right),\]

where \(M\) and \(K\) are the mean and Gaussian curvatures, respectively. This surface theory is not identical to the three-dimensional hard-wall volume model; it is a distinct approximation and should not be mixed with its numerical eigenvalues.

05 / Volume formulation

Three-dimensional toroidal confinement

Numerical formulation explored

The volume model retains all three spatial degrees of freedom inside \(\Omega\). The time-independent eigenproblem is

\[H_0\psi_n(\mathbf r)=E_n\psi_n(\mathbf r),\qquad \psi_n|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\]

In Cartesian coordinates, the kinetic term is the standard Laplacian. In cylindrical coordinates it is

\[\nabla^2=\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\!\left(\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)+\frac{1}{\rho^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}+\frac{\partial^2}{\partial z^2}.\]

The finite computational domain is represented by a grid or mesh, retaining points that satisfy the torus inequality. The hard-wall boundary is imposed by excluding points outside the domain or by an equivalent boundary treatment. The exact discretization, grid spacings, boundary stencil and Coulomb regularization need to accompany every reported spectrum.

06 / Computational approach

Numerical workflow

1. Construct the domain

Generate a Cartesian grid and retain nodes satisfying \(B(\rho,z)>0\). Record \(R\), \(a\), grid dimensions and spacings.

2. Assemble the Hamiltonian

Discretize the kinetic operator, add the Coulomb potential, and impose the Dirichlet boundary. Store the resulting operator sparsely.

3. Solve the stationary problem

Use a sparse eigensolver to obtain low-lying eigenpairs. Check residual norms, normalization and stability under grid refinement.

4. Prepare the initial state

Use a normalized stationary eigenstate, normally the ground state, as the initial condition for time propagation.

5. Propagate under a pulse

Introduce the time-dependent field and propagate the TDSE with a norm-stable method. Verify time-step and spatial-grid convergence.

6. Analyse observables

Evaluate density, energy, dipole response and probability current; only then calculate spectra and interpret the dynamics.

\[i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(\mathbf r,t)=H(t)\psi(\mathbf r,t).\]

Implementation note: a sparse Hamiltonian and an eigensolver were explored in the earlier computational work. A complete, public, reproducible code release and convergence report are still required before the numerical study can be independently reproduced.

08 / Ultrafast driving

Attosecond laser excitation

Pulse model explored

The time-dependent stage introduces an external electric field. In the length gauge and dipole approximation, a common Hamiltonian is

\[H(t)=H_0+\mathbf E(t)\cdot\mathbf r,\]

for an electron of charge \(-e\), with the sign convention depending on whether the interaction is written in terms of the electron charge or the field coupling. The convention must be fixed consistently in code and manuscript.

A Gaussian-envelope carrier pulse may be written as

\[\mathbf E(t)=\mathbf E_0\,f(t)\cos(\omega t+\varphi_{\mathrm{CEP}})\,\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon},\qquad f(t)=\exp\!\left[-4\ln2\,\frac{(t-t_0)^2}{T^2}\right],\]

where \(T\) is the intensity-envelope full width at half maximum under this convention, \(\omega\) is the carrier frequency and \(\varphi_{\mathrm{CEP}}\) is the carrier-envelope phase. The pulse shape, polarization and gauge are modelling choices, not yet a fully benchmarked result.

Pulse parameters in the development notes

A pulse duration of approximately 200 attoseconds was explored, recorded as \(\tau=8.3\) atomic units \(\approx200.77\) as. Other notes mention a 1,200 as pulse and a photon energy near 30 eV (\(\omega\approx1.1025\) a.u.). These refer to different exploratory parameter choices and should not be interpreted as one finalized simulation.

09 / Analysis plan

Observables and diagnostics

Probability density

\(\rho(\mathbf r,t)=|\psi(\mathbf r,t)|^2\). Visualize localization, redistribution and boundary effects.

Probability current

\(\mathbf j=(\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi)\) in the field-free Schrödinger representation; gauge-covariant form is needed with vector potentials.

Energy expectation

\(\langle H(t)\rangle=\langle\psi(t)|H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle\). Track energy exchange and numerical drift.

Dipole response

\(\langle\mathbf r(t)\rangle\) and its acceleration provide a route to analysing the emitted spectrum.

Additional quantities under consideration include angular-momentum expectation values, spin-resolved dynamics when spin interactions are explicitly included, ionization probability, high-harmonic spectra and phase-sensitive response. These are analysis targets, not established findings at this stage.

11 / Scientific transparency

Current limitations and open checks

  • Reproducibility: the historical energy values do not yet have a complete, publicly archived run configuration attached to them.
  • Convergence: systematic grid-refinement, time-step and solver-tolerance studies are still needed.
  • Coulomb singularity: the numerical treatment near the proton must be documented and tested for stability.
  • Model distinction: the surface-constrained and 3D-volume models describe different physical assumptions; their outputs must remain separate.
  • Laser propagation: norm conservation, gauge consistency, pulse calibration and time-step stability must be demonstrated.
  • Interpretation: claims about tunnelling, ionization, spin dynamics, Berry phase or high-harmonic generation require corresponding calculations and diagnostics before being reported as findings.
