Quantum Mechanics

Foundations, rigorous derivations, solved problems and research directions

Kumar Azad

M.Sc. Physics · SRMIST · Learning notes · 10 November 2026

Abstract

These notes organize graduate-level introductory quantum mechanics into five connected units: Hilbert spaces and operators, dynamics and quantum pictures, one-dimensional systems, central potentials and hydrogen, and symmetries and spin. Particular attention is given to calculations previously explored in problem-solving sessions: the infinite well, the harmonic-oscillator ladder, exact Heisenberg motion, and the integer azimuthal quantum number. Additional sections propose computational projects, including an exploratory model of hydrogen under attosecond excitation with idealized toroidal confinement. The emphasis throughout is on deriving equations from assumptions, checking their domains of validity, and distinguishing standard results from proposed investigations.

Status and scope. Educational mathematical-physics notes based on the SRMIST Quantum Mechanics syllabus (five units, 15 hours each; 75 instructional hours). The example calculations are established textbook results; the research-idea section proposes possible investigations, not completed experiments, original discoveries or accepted publications.

1. A rigorous roadmap for every quantum problem

Begin with a well-defined state space \(\mathcal H\), the observable or Hamiltonian and its domain, the physical assumptions (for example, fixed boundaries or an external classical field), and the initial and boundary conditions. Solve the eigenvalue problem or time evolution, normalize the states, and identify measurable observables. Check units, limiting cases, conservation laws and—when computing numerically—convergence.

\[ H|\psi_n\rangle=E_n|\psi_n\rangle,\qquad i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi(t)\rangle =H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle . \]

A self-adjoint Hamiltonian generates norm-preserving unitary evolution. An equation written without a suitable domain or specified boundary conditions may not uniquely define the intended physical system.

Unit I · Formalism — 1 · 15 hours

2. State spaces, observables and quantum postulates

Experimental motivations include blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, double-slit interference, matter-wave diffraction and polarization. The mathematical leap is from a classical state specified by coordinates and momenta to a normalized vector or ray in a complex Hilbert space. In position representation for a spinless particle in one dimension, the state space is \(L^2(\mathbb R,dx)\).

\[ \langle \phi|\psi\rangle= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi^*(x)\psi(x)\,dx, \qquad \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1 . \]

The expectation of a self-adjoint observable \(A\), for normalized \(|\psi\rangle\) in the relevant domain, is \(\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle\). If \(A\) has a discrete nondegenerate eigenbasis \(|a_n\rangle\), a projective measurement has probability \(P(a_n)=|\langle a_n|\psi\rangle|^2\). Continuous spectra require probability densities or spectral measures.

2.1 Operators, commutators and uncertainty

\[ \hat x\psi=x\psi,\qquad \hat p\psi=-i\hbar\,\frac{d\psi}{dx}, \qquad [\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar I . \]

Define centered observables \(\Delta A=A-\langle A\rangle\) and \(\Delta B=B-\langle B\rangle\). Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the vectors \(\Delta A|\psi\rangle\) and \(\Delta B|\psi\rangle\), then retain the imaginary part of their inner product. This proves the Robertson inequality:

\[ (\Delta A)_\psi(\Delta B)_\psi \geq\frac12\left|\langle[A,B]\rangle_\psi\right|, \qquad \Delta x\,\Delta p\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}. \]

The stronger Schrödinger–Robertson inequality additionally includes the covariance term. Uncertainty describes state-dependent dispersions, not simply instrumental error or the effect of a measurement.

Unit II · Formalism — 2 · 15 hours

3. Schrödinger evolution, probability and pictures

For a time-independent self-adjoint Hamiltonian, the solution is obtained using the unitary propagator. For time-dependent \(H(t)\), \(U(t,t_0)\) instead satisfies a first-order operator differential equation and is generally a time-ordered exponential.

\[ |\psi_{\mathrm S}(t)\rangle =U(t,t_0)|\psi_{\mathrm S}(t_0)\rangle,\qquad U(t,t_0)=e^{-iH(t-t_0)/\hbar}\quad(H\ \text{time independent}). \]

Define the Heisenberg operator by a unitary change of picture. For a Schrödinger operator \(A_{\mathrm S}(t)\),

\[ \begin{aligned} A_{\mathrm H}(t)&=U^\dagger(t,t_0)A_{\mathrm S}(t)U(t,t_0),\\ \frac{dA_{\mathrm H}}{dt} &=\frac{i}{\hbar}[H_{\mathrm H},A_{\mathrm H}] +\left(\frac{\partial A_{\mathrm S}}{\partial t}\right)_{\!\mathrm H}. \end{aligned} \]

Physical predictions are picture-independent because \(\langle\psi_{\mathrm S}(t)|A_{\mathrm S}|\psi_{\mathrm S}(t)\rangle=\langle\psi_{\mathrm S}(t_0)|A_{\mathrm H}(t)|\psi_{\mathrm S}(t_0)\rangle\). For perturbation theory, the interaction picture splits \(H=H_0+V(t)\); the interaction-picture state obeys \(i\hbar\partial_t|\psi_{\mathrm I}\rangle=V_{\mathrm I}(t)|\psi_{\mathrm I}\rangle\), with \(V_{\mathrm I}=e^{iH_0(t-t_0)/\hbar}V(t)e^{-iH_0(t-t_0)/\hbar}\) when \(H_0\) is constant.

3.1 Conservation of probability

Write \(i\hbar\partial_t\psi=[-\hbar^2\nabla^2/(2m)+V]\psi\) with real \(V\). Multiply by \(\psi^*\), subtract the conjugate equation multiplied by \(\psi\), and rearrange:

\[ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0, \qquad \rho=|\psi|^2,\qquad \mathbf j=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi). \]

For a particle in an external vector potential, the gauge-covariant current differs from this field-free expression. Wave packets, minimum-uncertainty Gaussians, the statistical interpretation, Ehrenfest's theorem and the equivalence of quantum pictures belong to the same unit.

Unit III · One-dimensional systems · 15 hours

4. Wells, tunnelling, oscillators and periodic potentials

A one-dimensional model replaces the full spatial domain by a line, interval or periodic coordinate. The boundary conditions are part of the problem, not an afterthought. Central examples include free motion, potential steps, reflection and transmission, finite and infinite wells, the attractive delta potential, tunnelling and Bloch's theorem.

4.1 The infinite square well

For \(V=0\) on \(0

\[ \phi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), \qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2},\quad n=1,2,\ldots . \]

Normalization follows from \(\int_0^L\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,dx=L/2\). The state cannot have zero kinetic energy under these boundary conditions. In every stationary state, \(\langle x\rangle=L/2\) and \(\langle p\rangle=0\) for the standard differential momentum expression; the self-adjoint-domain subtleties of momentum on a finite interval merit separate study.

4.2 An attractive delta potential

For \(V(x)=-\lambda\delta(x)\) with \(\lambda>0\), integrate the stationary Schrödinger equation across \(x=0\). The wavefunction remains continuous while its derivative has a prescribed jump:

\[ \psi'(0^+)-\psi'(0^-) =-\frac{2m\lambda}{\hbar^2}\psi(0),\qquad \psi_b(x)=\sqrt{\kappa}e^{-\kappa|x|}, \quad \kappa=\frac{m\lambda}{\hbar^2},\quad E_b=-\frac{m\lambda^2}{2\hbar^2}. \]

The bound state is the unique square-integrable state for this attractive point interaction. For a finite rectangular barrier \(V_0>E>0\) of width \(a\), the inverse decay length is \(\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\), and transmission is nonzero for finite \(a\):

\[ T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)} {4E(V_0-E)}\right]^{-1},\qquad 0

For periodic potentials \(V(x+a)=V(x)\), Bloch's theorem changes the boundary structure: \(\psi_k(x+a)=e^{ika}\psi_k(x)\), and solutions are organized into energy bands.

4.3 Quantum harmonic oscillator — the algebraic route

For the confining Hamiltonian \(H=p^2/(2m)+m\omega^2x^2/2\), define operators that combine the canonical variables:

\[ \begin{gathered} a=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\,x +\frac{i\,p}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}},\qquad a^\dagger=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\,x -\frac{i\,p}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}},\\ [a,a^\dagger]=1,\qquad H=\hbar\omega\left(a^\dagger a+\frac12\right),\qquad E_n=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac12\right). \end{gathered} \]

Because \(N=a^\dagger a\) is nonnegative, the ladder has a lowest state with \(a|0\rangle=0\). In position space this is a first-order differential equation, yielding

\[ \psi_0(x)= \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4} \exp\left(-\frac{m\omega x^2}{2\hbar}\right), \qquad \Delta x\,\Delta p=\frac{\hbar}{2}. \]

Unit IV · Three-dimensional systems · 15 hours

5. Angular momentum, spherical harmonics and hydrogen

For a central potential \(V(r)\), the Hamiltonian commutes with \(L^2\) and \(L_z\). In spherical coordinates, the azimuthal factor of a separated wavefunction satisfies

\[ L_z=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi},\qquad L_z\Phi=\hbar m\Phi,\qquad \Phi(\varphi)=e^{im\varphi}. \]

Single-valuedness for an ordinary scalar orbital wavefunction gives \(\Phi(\varphi+2\pi)=\Phi(\varphi)\). Therefore \(e^{i2\pi m}=1\) and \(m\in\mathbb Z\). The polar equation restricts \(\ell\) to \(0,1,2,\ldots\) with \(|m|\leq\ell\). A normalized angular basis is supplied by the spherical harmonics \(Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\).

\[ L^2Y_{\ell m}=\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)Y_{\ell m}, \qquad L_zY_{\ell m}=\hbar mY_{\ell m}, \qquad [L_i,L_j]=i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}L_k . \]

5.1 Hydrogen radial equation

Assume a fixed Coulomb center of charge \(+Ze\), or account for finite nuclear mass by using the electron–nucleus reduced mass \(\mu\). The relative-coordinate Hamiltonian is \(H=-\hbar^2\Delta/(2\mu)-Ze^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r)\). Set \(\psi=R_{n\ell}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\) and \(u_{n\ell}=rR_{n\ell}\). The PDE reduces to

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} +\left[\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2} -\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\right]u=Eu, \qquad r>0 . \]

Regularity at the origin and square integrability at infinity select the discrete bound-state spectrum. With principal quantum number \(n=1,2,\ldots\), \(\ell=0,\ldots,n-1\), the nonrelativistic Coulomb energies are

\[ E_n=-\frac{\mu Z^2e^4} {2(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2n^2}, \qquad a_\mu=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{\mu e^2}. \]

For \(Z=1\), the normalized \(1s\) relative-coordinate wavefunction is \(\psi_{100}(\mathbf r)=e^{-r/a_\mu}/\sqrt{\pi a_\mu^3}\). The probability of finding the electron between \(r\) and \(r+dr\) is \(4\pi r^2|\psi_{100}(r)|^2dr\); the \(r^2\) Jacobian is essential. The Coulomb problem illustrates angular-momentum representation theory, special functions, boundary-value analysis and degeneracy in a single system.

These formulas omit fine structure, Lamb shifts, hyperfine structure and external fields. The reduced-mass formulation is a two-body approximation; an interacting helium atom does not have an analogous elementary closed-form spectrum.

Unit V · Symmetries and conservation · 15 hours

6. Spatial symmetries, spin, and time reversal

Translations, rotations, inversion and time reversal constrain physical observables. If a time-independent Hamiltonian commutes with an operator \(A\) with no explicit time dependence, its expectation is conserved. A rotationally invariant Hamiltonian obeys \([H,\mathbf L]=0\) componentwise; this is why angular momentum labels are useful in central potentials.

6.1 Spin-\(\frac12\), SU(2) and Pauli matrices

For a spin-\(\tfrac12\) degree of freedom, a normalized state is a two-component spinor. Its components are probability amplitudes for an orthonormal spin basis, not ordinary three-dimensional spatial directions.

\[ \sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \sigma_y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}. \]
\[ S_i=\frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i,\qquad [\sigma_i,\sigma_j]=2i\varepsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k,\qquad U(\hat{\mathbf n},\theta)= \exp\left(-\frac{i\theta}{2}\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\right). \]

The distinction between \(SU(2)\) and spatial \(SO(3)\) rotations explains why a \(2\pi\) rotation multiplies a spin-\(\tfrac12\) spinor by \(-1\), while ordinary measured probabilities remain unchanged. This spinor behavior is not in conflict with the single-valuedness condition used for the orbital factor \(e^{im\varphi}\).

6.2 Kramers degeneracy

For a spin-\(\tfrac12\) state with time-reversal operator \(\Theta=i\sigma_yK\) (with \(K\) complex conjugation), one has \(\Theta^2=-1\). If a Hamiltonian is invariant under this time reversal, each eigenstate has an orthogonal partner at the same energy. This is the content of Kramers degeneracy, subject to the appropriate half-integer-spin and symmetry assumptions; an external magnetic field can break time-reversal symmetry.

7. Worked derivations revisited

The following are short derivation records from topics studied in the quantum-mechanics problem sessions. Each is an educational worked example, not a statement that an entire assignment or textbook has been reproduced here.

7.1 Exact Heisenberg motion of the harmonic oscillator

From \(H=p^2/(2m)+m\omega^2x^2/2\), the Heisenberg equations give two coupled operator ODEs:

\[ \dot x_{\mathrm H}=\frac{p_{\mathrm H}}{m}, \qquad \dot p_{\mathrm H}=-m\omega^2x_{\mathrm H}. \]

Differentiate the first equation and substitute the second to obtain \(\ddot x_{\mathrm H}+\omega^2x_{\mathrm H}=0\). Using \(x_{\mathrm H}(0)=x\) and \(\dot x_{\mathrm H}(0)=p/m\), and then differentiating, gives the exact result:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_{\mathrm H}(t)&=x\cos(\omega t) +\frac{p}{m\omega}\sin(\omega t),\\ p_{\mathrm H}(t)&=p\cos(\omega t) -m\omega x\sin(\omega t). \end{aligned} \]

The canonical commutator is preserved because

\[ [x_{\mathrm H}(t),p_{\mathrm H}(t)] =(\cos^2\omega t+\sin^2\omega t)[x,p] =i\hbar . \]

Taking expectation values reproduces the classical oscillator equations for \(\langle x\rangle\) and \(\langle p\rangle\) (Ehrenfest dynamics). In a number state these means vanish; in a coherent state they oscillate. The same operator solution follows from \(a_{\mathrm H}(t)=ae^{-i\omega t}\).

7.2 A dimensional check of the infinite well

In \(E_n=n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)\), the factor \(\hbar^2/(mL^2)\) has units of energy. The limit \(L\to\infty\) drives the level spacing toward zero, as expected when the confining walls separate indefinitely. The normalization factor \(\sqrt{2/L}\) has units \(L^{-1/2}\) in one dimension.

7.3 Angular single-valuedness and quantum numbers

Claim. For scalar orbital wavefunctions on ordinary three-dimensional space, the azimuthal eigenvalue label must be an integer.

Proof. Solve \(L_z\Phi=\hbar m\Phi\), obtaining \(\Phi(\varphi)=Ce^{im\varphi}\). Since the points \(\varphi\) and \(\varphi+2\pi\) coincide in ordinary spherical coordinates, \(\Phi(\varphi+2\pi)=\Phi(\varphi)\). Therefore \(e^{2\pi im}=1\), so \(m\in\mathbb Z\). Additional regularity determines \(\ell\) and the range \(-\ell\leq m\leq\ell\). This proof concerns the scalar orbital factor and does not prohibit half-integer intrinsic spin.

8.1 Proposed model: hydrogen under an attosecond pulse in a toroidal region

One exploratory direction considers an electron in a bounded three-dimensional solid torus, subject to a Coulomb attraction to a fixed proton and a prescribed electromagnetic pulse. The toroidal shape does not turn the system into a one-dimensional problem. In Cartesian coordinates, specify the region by a major radius \(R>a>0\):

\[ \Omega_{R,a}= \left\{(x,y,z): \left(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}-R\right)^2+z^2

Choose a proton position \(\mathbf r_0\in\Omega_{R,a}\), a normalized initial electronic state, and an idealized Dirichlet boundary condition \(\psi|_{\partial\Omega}=0\). Within the length-gauge electric-dipole approximation for an electron of charge \(-e\), a possible model is

\[ \begin{gathered} H(t)=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\Delta_{\Omega} -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|} +e\,\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf E(t),\\ i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi=H(t)\psi,\qquad \psi|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\qquad \|\psi(0)\|_{L^2(\Omega)}=1 . \end{gathered} \]

The boundary and field idealizations must be justified. The reference energy and dipole origin should be chosen consistently; a spatially uniform additive time-dependent potential contributes only a global phase. The rigorous starting point is the domain of the static Coulomb Hamiltonian and the behavior of its spectrum—not a premature claim about predicted ionization or experimental observability.

Research pathway: five checks before any paper claim
  1. Define the geometry, nuclear location, field profile, boundary conditions and units. Distinguish a solid torus from a thin toroidal surface.
  2. Study the field-free Hamiltonian and compute its low-energy states with mesh/basis convergence and boundary-error checks.
  3. Set \(\mathbf E(t)=0\) as a validation limit; vary \(R\) and \(a\) and compare with physically appropriate unconfined or limiting geometries, accounting for the nucleus's placement.
  4. Switch on a prescribed pulse, monitor norm conservation for a closed Hermitian model, and compare perturbative and nonperturbative regimes where applicable.
  5. Perform a literature search and explicitly identify what the model contributes beyond established confinement and attosecond calculations. Do not infer publication novelty from model complexity.

A first MSc-level benchmark should be simpler: hydrogen in free space under a weak electric pulse, or a one-dimensional driven oscillator with an exact solution. Once that calculation is correct and reproducible, complexity can be added one assumption at a time.

10. Exercises and further reading

Practice problems. (i) Derive \(\Delta x\Delta p\geq\hbar/2\) from Cauchy–Schwarz. (ii) Normalize the infinite-well eigenfunctions and compute \(\langle x\rangle\). (iii) Derive the delta-potential derivative jump. (iv) Obtain the oscillator ground state from \(a\psi_0=0\). (v) Verify the exact Heisenberg commutator at arbitrary \(t\). (vi) Prove integer orbital \(m\) using periodicity in \(\varphi\). (vii) Derive \([\sigma_x,\sigma_y]=2i\sigma_z\). (viii) Specify a convergence test for a proposed time-dependent hydrogen simulation.
