A1Verify Cauchy–Schwarz for two Legendre functions
Question as transcribed in manuscript

Verify the Schwarz inequality on \([-1,1]\) for \(f_1(x)=x\) and \(f_2(x)=\tfrac12(3x^2-1)\).

Detailed worked solution

Step 1: Set up the inner product. Use \(\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-1}^{1}f^*(x)g(x)\,\dd x\). The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is

\[\abs{\langle f_1,f_2\rangle}^2\leq\langle f_1,f_1\rangle\langle f_2,f_2\rangle.\]

Step 2: Compute each norm exactly.

\[\begin{aligned}\langle f_1,f_1\rangle&=\int_{-1}^{1}x^2\,\dd x=\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^{1}=\frac23,\\ \langle f_2,f_2\rangle&=\frac14\int_{-1}^{1}(9x^4-6x^2+1)\,\dd x\nonumber\\ &=\frac14\left(9\cdot\frac25-6\cdot\frac23+2\right)=\frac25.\end{aligned}\]

Step 3: Compute the mixed scalar product.

\[\langle f_1,f_2\rangle=\frac12\int_{-1}^{1}(3x^3-x)\,\dd x=0,\]

because the integrand is odd on a symmetric interval. Consequently

\[\abs{\langle f_1,f_2\rangle}^2=0\leq \frac23\cdot\frac25=\frac4{15}.\]
\[\boxed{0\leq 4/15\quad\text{(verified).}}\]

Interpretation. \(f_1=P_1\) and \(f_2=P_2\) are distinct Legendre polynomials. Their orthogonality is a special case of \(\int_{-1}^{1}P_\ell P_j\,\dd x=2\delta_{\ell j}/(2\ell+1)\). Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz would require linear dependence; these nonzero functions are linearly independent, so the inequality is strict.

A2Compatibility of momentum and Hamiltonian for a free particle
Question as transcribed in manuscript

Show that for a free particle in one dimension momentum and Hamiltonian are compatible observables, whereas position and Hamiltonian are not.

Detailed worked solution

Step 1: Write the differential operators. For a free particle on the full real line,

\[\hat p=-\iu\hbar\frac{\dd}{\dd x},\quad \hat x=x,\quad\hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2}.\]

Step 2: Calculate \([\hat p,\hat H]\) directly. On a smooth test function \(\phi\),

\[\begin{aligned}\hat p\hat H\phi&=(-\iu\hbar)\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\right)\phi'''=\frac{\iu\hbar^3}{2m}\phi''',\\ \hat H\hat p\phi&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(-\iu\hbar\phi')''=\frac{\iu\hbar^3}{2m}\phi'''.\end{aligned}\]

Thus \([\hat p,\hat H]\phi=0\) and

\[\boxed{[\hat p,\hat H]=0.}\]

As \(\hat H=p^2/(2m)\) in the momentum representation, \(\hat H\) and \(\hat p\) are simultaneously diagonalized by the Fourier transform. Their generalized common eigenfunctions \(\ee^{\iu px/\hbar}\) have \(E=p^2/(2m)\); the energy \(E>0\) corresponds to \(p=\pm\sqrt{2mE}\).

Step 3: Calculate \([\hat x,\hat H]\). The product rule gives

\[\begin{aligned}\hat x\hat H\phi&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}x\phi'',\\ \hat H\hat x\phi&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(x\phi)''=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(2\phi'+x\phi'').\end{aligned}\]

Subtract:

\[[\hat x,\hat H]\phi=\frac{\hbar^2}{m}\phi'=\frac{\iu\hbar}{m}\hat p\phi.\]

Therefore,

\[\boxed{[\hat p,\hat H]=0,\qquad[\hat x,\hat H]=\frac{\iu\hbar}{m}\hat p\neq0.}\]

Step 4: Physical meaning. Momentum and energy are compatible observables for free motion, while position and energy generally are not. The Heisenberg equations reinforce this result:

\[\frac{\dd\hat p}{\dd t}=\frac{1}{\iu\hbar}[\hat p,\hat H]=0,\qquad \frac{\dd\hat x}{\dd t}=\frac{1}{\iu\hbar}[\hat x,\hat H]=\frac{\hat p}{m}.\]

The first is momentum conservation; the second is the quantum analog of uniform rectilinear motion.

A3Finding the particle in the central half of an infinite box
Question as transcribed in manuscript

Find the probability of detecting a particle between \(x=L/4\) and \(x=3L/4\) in the ground state and the first excited state of a one-dimensional infinite square well.

Detailed worked solution

Step 1: Derive the stationary eigenfunctions. For \(V=0\) in \((0,L)\), the Schrödinger equation reads \(\psi''+k^2\psi=0\). The general solution \(A\sin kx+B\cos kx\) obeys \(\psi(0)=0\), forcing \(B=0\), and \(\psi(L)=0\), forcing \(kL=n\pi\) for \(n\geq1\). Normalization follows from

\[1=\abs{A}^2\int_0^L\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,\dd x=\abs A^2\frac L2.\]

Therefore

\[\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\quad 0<x<L;\qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}.\]

Step 2: Apply Born's probability rule. The probability of the specified interval is

\[P_n=\int_{L/4}^{3L/4}\abs{\psi_n(x)}^2\,\dd x=\frac2L\int_{L/4}^{3L/4}\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\dd x.\]

Using \(2\sin^2\theta=1-\cos 2\theta\) gives the antiderivative

\[P_n=\left[\frac{x}{L}-\frac{\sin(2n\pi x/L)}{2n\pi}\right]_{L/4}^{3L/4} =\frac12-\frac{\sin(3n\pi/2)-\sin(n\pi/2)}{2n\pi}.\]

Step 3: Ground state \(n=1\). Since \(\sin(3\pi/2)=-1\) and \(\sin(\pi/2)=1\),

\[P_1=\frac12-\frac{-1-1}{2\pi}=\boxed{\frac12+\frac1\pi\simeq0.81831}.\]

Step 4: First excited state \(n=2\). Since \(\sin(3\pi)=\sin\pi=0\),

\[P_2=\frac12-\frac{0-0}{4\pi}=\boxed{\frac12}.\]
\[\boxed{P_{\text{ground}}=81.83\%,\qquad P_{\text{first excited}}=50\%.}\]

The ground-state density peaks at \(L/2\), whereas the first excited state has a node there, explaining the different central-interval probabilities.

A4Equal superposition of two infinite-well eigenstates
Question as transcribed in manuscript

A particle in an infinite square well of length \(L\) is in the state \(\psi(x)=A[\psi_1(x)+\psi_2(x)]\). Determine \(A\), \(\langle H\rangle\) and the energy uncertainty \(\sigma_H\).

Detailed worked solution

The normalized eigenstates satisfy \(\langle\psi_m,\psi_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\) and \(\hat H\psi_n=E_n\psi_n\), with \(E_n=n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)\). Step 1: Normalization.

\[\begin{aligned}1&=\langle\psi,\psi\rangle =\abs A^2\left(\langle\psi_1,\psi_1\rangle+\langle\psi_1,\psi_2\rangle+\langle\psi_2,\psi_1\rangle+\langle\psi_2,\psi_2\rangle\right)\\ &=2\abs A^2.\end{aligned}\]

Thus \(\abs A=1/\sqrt2\); choosing the arbitrary global phase to be zero, \(A=1/\sqrt2\). Step 2: Expectation of energy. Using \(\hat H\psi_n=E_n\psi_n\),

\[\begin{aligned}\langle H\rangle&=\frac12\langle\psi_1+\psi_2,\hat H(\psi_1+\psi_2)\rangle\\ &=\frac12\langle\psi_1+\psi_2,E_1\psi_1+E_2\psi_2\rangle=\frac{E_1+E_2}{2}.\end{aligned}\]

Since \(E_2=4E_1\),

\[\boxed{\langle H\rangle=\frac52E_1=\frac{5\pi^2\hbar^2}{4mL^2}.}\]

Step 3: Second moment and variance. Acting twice with the Hamiltonian gives \(\hat H^2\psi_n=E_n^2\psi_n\), hence

\[\begin{aligned}\langle H^2\rangle&=\frac{E_1^2+E_2^2}{2}=\frac{17}{2}E_1^2=\frac{17\pi^4\hbar^4}{8m^2L^4},\\ \sigma_H^2&=\langle H^2\rangle-\langle H\rangle^2=\left(\frac{17}{2}-\frac{25}{4}\right)E_1^2=\frac94E_1^2.\end{aligned}\]

The nonnegative standard deviation is \(\sigma_H=(3/2)E_1\).

\[\boxed{A=\frac1{\sqrt2},\qquad\langle H\rangle=\frac{5\pi^2\hbar^2}{4mL^2},\qquad\sigma_H=\frac{3\pi^2\hbar^2}{4mL^2}.}\]

Interpretation. The energy probabilities are \(P(E_1)=P(E_2)=1/2\), so \(\sigma_H=(E_2-E_1)/2\). For the time-evolving superposition

\[\Psi(x,t)=\frac{\psi_1(x)\ee^{-\iu E_1t/\hbar}+\psi_2(x)\ee^{-\iu E_2t/\hbar}}{\sqrt2},\]

the position probability density oscillates through interference, but the energy distribution and its standard deviation remain time independent.

A5Griffiths Problem 3.27 — verified second-edition interpretation
Question as transcribed in manuscript

The assignment reads only ``Problem 3.27 Griffiths,'' without stating the edition. Here we solve the verified second-edition (2005) version on sequential measurements. A full solution to the distinct first-edition and third-edition versions is supplied in the edition-specific supporting derivations below.

Detailed worked solution

Edition note. The second-edition printed problem is titled ``Sequential measurements'' and uses the two bases and coefficients below (see references). The first edition instead asks for Gaussian-function Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization (see references); the third edition asks for spectral decomposition of operators and operator exponentials (see references). No edition can be established solely from the uploaded assignment.

Let the nondegenerate eigenbases of the observables \(\hat A\) and \(\hat B\) be \(\{\ket{\psi_1},\ket{\psi_2}\}\) and \(\{\ket{\phi_1},\ket{\phi_2}\}\), with

\[\ket{\psi_1}=\frac35\ket{\phi_1}+\frac45\ket{\phi_2},\qquad \ket{\psi_2}=\frac45\ket{\phi_1}-\frac35\ket{\phi_2}.\]

Step 1: Postmeasurement state of \(A\). If the ideal projective measurement of \(A\) produces \(a_1\), then immediately afterward the state is \(\boxed{\ket{\psi_1}}\).

Step 2: Measurement of \(B\). From Born's rule and orthonormality of the \(B\)-basis,

\[\begin{aligned}P(b_1\mid a_1)&=\abs{\braket{\phi_1}{\psi_1}}^2=\left(\frac35\right)^2=\frac9{25},\\ P(b_2\mid a_1)&=\abs{\braket{\phi_2}{\psi_1}}^2=\left(\frac45\right)^2=\frac{16}{25}.\end{aligned}\]

Step 3: Reverse the basis transformation. In matrix form, \(U=\frac15\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}3&4\\4&-3\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\); one checks \(U^\dagger U=I\) and \(U^{-1}=U\). Thus

\[\ket{\phi_1}=\frac35\ket{\psi_1}+\frac45\ket{\psi_2},\qquad \ket{\phi_2}=\frac45\ket{\psi_1}-\frac35\ket{\psi_2}.\]

If the unreported \(B\)-outcome was \(b_1\), the final probability of \(a_1\) is \(9/25\); if it was \(b_2\), that conditional probability is \(16/25\). Because the \(B\) measurement actually occurred, its unrecorded alternatives add as probabilities, not probability amplitudes:

\[\begin{aligned}P(a_1\text{ on final }A\mid a_1\text{ initially}) &=\frac9{25}\cdot\frac9{25}+\frac{16}{25}\cdot\frac{16}{25}\\ &=\frac{81+256}{625}=\boxed{\frac{337}{625}=0.5392}.\end{aligned}\]

Density-operator verification. The intermediate unread measurement produces

\[\rho_B=\frac9{25}\ket{\phi_1}\bra{\phi_1}+\frac{16}{25}\ket{\phi_2}\bra{\phi_2}, \quad P(a_1)=\langle\psi_1|\rho_B|\psi_1\rangle=\frac{337}{625}.\]

If the measurement of \(B\) had been omitted entirely, an immediate repeat measurement of \(A\) would instead yield \(a_1\) with probability one. The intermediate measurement disturbs the state.

B1Generalized uncertainty relation and position–momentum application
Question as transcribed in manuscript

Derive the generalized uncertainty relation. Apply it to the position and momentum operators in one dimension.

Detailed worked solution

Step 1: Define centered observables and variances. Let \(\hat A\) and \(\hat B\) be self-adjoint observables in a normalized state \(\ket\psi\). Define

\[\delta\hat A=\hat A-\langle A\rangle\id,\quad \delta\hat B=\hat B-\langle B\rangle\id,\qquad (\Delta A)^2=\langle(\delta\hat A)^2\rangle,\quad(\Delta B)^2=\langle(\delta\hat B)^2\rangle.\]

For observables with finite variances, these expressions are nonnegative because they are squared Hilbert-space norms.

Step 2: Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to fluctuation vectors. Let

\[\ket u=\delta\hat A\ket\psi,\qquad \ket v=\delta\hat B\ket\psi.\]

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality states \(\langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle\geq\abs{\langle u,v\rangle}^2\). Therefore

\[\label{eq:cs_unc} \boxed{(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2\geq\abs{\langle\delta\hat A\,\delta\hat B\rangle}^2.}\]

Step 3: Separate symmetric and antisymmetric operator products. Define the commutator and anticommutator

\[[\hat A,\hat B]=\hat A\hat B-\hat B\hat A,\qquad\{\hat A,\hat B\}=\hat A\hat B+\hat B\hat A.\]

Then

\[\delta\hat A\delta\hat B=\frac12\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}+\frac12[\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B] =\frac12\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}+\frac12[\hat A,\hat B].\]

Self-adjointness of the centered observables implies that \(\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\) is Hermitian (real expectation) and \([\hat A,\hat B]\) is anti-Hermitian (purely imaginary expectation). Thus the two pieces are orthogonal as real and imaginary components of a complex number:

\[\begin{aligned}\abs{\langle\delta\hat A\delta\hat B\rangle}^2 =\frac14\abs{\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle}^2 +\frac14\abs{\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle}^2.\end{aligned}\]

Step 4: Obtain the Schrödinger–Robertson inequality. Substitution into (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:cs_unc) gives

\[\label{eq:SR} \boxed{(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2\geq\frac14\abs{\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle}^2+ \frac14\abs{\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle}^2.}\]

The second term is the square of the symmetrized covariance, \(\Cov(A,B)=\tfrac12\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle\). Omitting this nonnegative term gives the more familiar Robertson relation

\[\label{eq:robertson} \boxed{\Delta A\,\Delta B\geq\frac12\abs{\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle}.}\]

Step 5: Calculate the canonical commutator. In one dimension,

\[\hat x\phi=x\phi,\qquad\hat p\phi=-\iu\hbar\phi',\]

so on a smooth test function

\[\begin{aligned}[\hat x,\hat p]\phi&=x(-\iu\hbar\phi')-[-\iu\hbar(x\phi)']\\ &=-\iu\hbar x\phi'+\iu\hbar(\phi+x\phi')=\iu\hbar\phi.\end{aligned}\]

Hence \([\hat x,\hat p]=\iu\hbar\id\). Apply (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:robertson):

\[\Delta x\,\Delta p\geq\frac12\abs{\langle\iu\hbar\id\rangle}=\frac\hbar2.\]
\[\boxed{\Delta x\,\Delta p\geq\frac\hbar2,\qquad (\Delta x)^2(\Delta p)^2\geq\frac{\hbar^2}4+\Cov(x,p)^2.}\]

Step 6: Exhibit a minimum-uncertainty state. For \(\sigma>0\), consider

\[\psi(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{1/4}}\exp\left[-\frac{(x-x_0)^2}{4\sigma^2}+\frac{\iu p_0 x}{\hbar}\right].\]

Its Gaussian probability density has \(\langle x\rangle=x_0\), \(\Delta x=\sigma\), and direct differentiation yields

\[(\hat p-p_0)\psi=\frac{\iu\hbar(x-x_0)}{2\sigma^2}\psi,\quad \langle p\rangle=p_0,\quad (\Delta p)^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{4\sigma^2}.\]

Thus \(\Delta x\Delta p=\hbar/2\): the lower bound is attainable for a suitable Gaussian state. Equality in the full Schrödinger–Robertson inequality occurs exactly when the two centered fluctuation vectors are linearly dependent; equality in the simpler Robertson form additionally requires zero symmetrized covariance.

B2Potential step: energy above the step (\(E>V_0\))
Question as transcribed in manuscript

Derive the reflection and transmission probabilities for a particle with \(E>V_0\) incident on a potential step of height \(V_0\) at the origin.

Detailed worked solution

Let \(V(x)=0\) for \(x<0\) and \(V(x)=V_0>0\) for \(x\geq0\). With \(E>V_0\), both regional kinetic energies are positive. Define

\[k_1=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar},\qquad k_2=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}}{\hbar}.\]

\textbf{Step 1: Solve the two regional Schrödinger equations.} In Region I, \(\psi_I''+k_1^2\psi_I=0\); in Region II, \(\psi_{II}''+k_2^2\psi_{II}=0\). An incident wave from the left has the form

\[\psi_I=A\ee^{\iu k_1x}+B\ee^{-\iu k_1x},\qquad \psi_{II}=C\ee^{\iu k_2x}.\]

The coefficient of \(\ee^{-\iu k_2x}\) is zero because there is no incoming particle from the right.

Step 2: Match the wavefunction and derivative. The potential is finite at \(x=0\), so both \(\psi\) and \(\psi'\) are continuous:

\[A+B=C,\qquad \iu k_1(A-B)=\iu k_2C.\]

Eliminate \(C\) using \(C=A+B\):

\[k_1(A-B)=k_2(A+B)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(k_1-k_2)A=(k_1+k_2)B.\]

Thus the reflection and transmission amplitude ratios are

\[\boxed{r=\frac BA=\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2},\qquad t=\frac CA=1+r=\frac{2k_1}{k_1+k_2}.}\]

Step 3: Derive current coefficients. From the probability continuity equation,

\[\label{eq:current} j=\frac{\hbar}{2m\iu}\left(\psi^*\psi'-\psi(\psi^*)'\right).\]

For a right-moving plane wave \(F\ee^{\iu kx}\), \(j=(\hbar k/m)\abs F^2\); for a left-moving plane wave, \(j=-(\hbar k/m)\abs F^2\). Thus

\[j_{\rm inc}=\frac{\hbar k_1}{m}\abs A^2,\quad j_{\rm ref}=-\frac{\hbar k_1}{m}\abs B^2,\quad j_{\rm tr}=\frac{\hbar k_2}{m}\abs C^2.\]

The probabilities are ratios of the magnitudes of outgoing currents to incoming current:

\[\begin{aligned}R&=\frac{\abs{j_{\rm ref}}}{j_{\rm inc}}=\abs{\frac BA}^2=\boxed{\frac{(k_1-k_2)^2}{(k_1+k_2)^2}},\\ T&=\frac{j_{\rm tr}}{j_{\rm inc}}=\frac{k_2}{k_1}\abs{\frac CA}^2=\boxed{\frac{4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2}}.\end{aligned}\]

Step 4: Write the results in energy variables. The common factor \(\sqrt{2m}/\hbar\) cancels:

\[\boxed{R=\left(\frac{\sqrt E-\sqrt{E-V_0}}{\sqrt E+\sqrt{E-V_0}}\right)^2,\quad T=\frac{4\sqrt{E(E-V_0)}}{(\sqrt E+\sqrt{E-V_0})^2}.}\]

Step 5: Verify flux conservation.

\[R+T=\frac{(k_1-k_2)^2+4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2}=\frac{(k_1+k_2)^2}{(k_1+k_2)^2}=1.\]
\[\boxed{R+T=1.}\]

Interpretation and limits. Quantum reflection persists for \(E>V_0\) if \(k_1\ne k_2\), in contrast to a classical particle. If \(V_0\to0\), \(R\to0,T\to1\); if \(E\to V_0^+\), \(R\to1,T\to0\). In the high-energy limit, \(R\simeq V_0^2/(16E^2)\). Crucially, \(T\ne\abs t^2\) when incident and transmitted velocities differ.

B3Semi-infinite potential step: energy below the step (\(E<V_0\))
Question as transcribed in manuscript

Calculate the reflection and transmission probabilities for a particle with \(E<V_0\) incident on a potential step of height \(V_0\) at the origin.

Detailed worked solution

Here \(V(x)=0\) on \(x<0\) and \(V(x)=V_0\) on \(x\geq0\), with \(0<E<V_0\). Define

\[k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar},\qquad\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}.\]

Step 1: Regional differential equations. For \(x<0\), \(\psi_I''+k^2\psi_I=0\), hence \(\psi_I=A\ee^{\iu kx}+B\ee^{-\iu kx}\). For \(x>0\), \(\psi_{II}''-\kappa^2\psi_{II}=0\), hence generally \(C\ee^{-\kappa x}+D\ee^{\kappa x}\). Boundedness as \(x\to+\infty\) requires \(D=0\). Therefore

\[\boxed{\psi_I=A\ee^{\iu kx}+B\ee^{-\iu kx},\qquad\psi_{II}=C\ee^{-\kappa x}.}\]

Step 2: Match at the origin.

\[A+B=C,\qquad \iu k(A-B)=-\kappa C.\]

Substituting the first condition into the second yields

\[(\iu k+\kappa)A=(\iu k-\kappa)B,\]

so

\[\boxed{r=\frac BA=\frac{k-\iu\kappa}{k+\iu\kappa},\qquad \frac CA=1+r=\frac{2k}{k+\iu\kappa}.}\]

Step 3: Reflection probability. Using the same incident and reflected velocities,

\[R=\abs r^2=\frac{\abs{k-\iu\kappa}^2}{\abs{k+\iu\kappa}^2}=\frac{k^2+\kappa^2}{k^2+\kappa^2}=\boxed{1}.\]

Step 4: Transmission current. For \(\psi_{II}=C\ee^{-\kappa x}\), its derivative is \(\psi_{II}'=-\kappa\psi_{II}\), with real \(\kappa\). Equation (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:current) gives

\[j_{II}=\frac{\hbar}{2m\iu}[-\kappa\abs{\psi_{II}}^2+\kappa\abs{\psi_{II}}^2]=0.\]

Hence \(T=j_{II}/j_{\rm inc}=0\), despite \(C\ne0\).

\[\boxed{R=1,\qquad T=0,\qquad R+T=1.}\]

Step 5: Penetration depth. The amplitude decays as \(\ee^{-\kappa x}\). Its \(1/e\) amplitude penetration depth is

\[\boxed{\delta=\frac1\kappa=\frac\hbar{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}};\qquad \abs{\psi_{II}(x)}^2=\abs C^2\ee^{-2\kappa x}.\]

The probability-density decay length is \(1/(2\kappa)\). The amplitude ratio \(\abs{C/A}^2=4E/V_0\) is not a transmission probability; it can exceed unity because it is not a flux ratio. The finite forbidden-region case with nonzero transmission is treated next.

B4Exact tunneling through a finite rectangular barrier
Question as transcribed in manuscript

Derive the tunneling probability for a particle with \(0<E<V_0\) incident on a finite potential barrier of height \(V_0\) and width \(a\).

Detailed worked solution

The potential is \(V(x)=0\) for \(x<0\), \(V_0\) for \(0<x<a\), and \(0\) for \(x>a\). Define

\[k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar},\qquad \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar},\qquad k,\kappa>0.\]
The original LaTeX source contains the accompanying figure and its code.

Step 1: Solve the regional equations. The Schrödinger equation gives propagating solutions outside and exponential solutions inside:

\[\label{eq:barrier_waves} \boxed{\begin{aligned} \psi_I&=A\ee^{\iu kx}+B\ee^{-\iu kx}&&(x<0),\\ \psi_{II}&=C\ee^{\kappa x}+D\ee^{-\kappa x}&&(0<x<a),\\ \psi_{III}&=F\ee^{\iu kx}&&(x>a). \end{aligned}}\]

The \(\ee^{-\iu kx}\) term is absent in Region III because there is no incidence from the right. Both exponentials must be retained in Region II: there are two interfaces to match.

Step 2: Write the four boundary equations. Matching \(\psi\) and \(\psi'\) at \(x=0\) gives

\[A+B=C+D,\qquad \iu k(A-B)=\kappa(C-D).\]

At \(x=a\) this gives

\[C\ee^{\kappa a}+D\ee^{-\kappa a}=F\ee^{\iu ka},\quad \kappa(C\ee^{\kappa a}-D\ee^{-\kappa a})=\iu kF\ee^{\iu ka}.\]

Step 3: Solve the interface at \(a\). Add and subtract the last equations to isolate \(C\ee^{\kappa a}\) and \(D\ee^{-\kappa a}\):

\[\begin{aligned}C&=\frac F2\ee^{\iu ka-\kappa a}\left(1+\frac{\iu k}{\kappa}\right),\\ D&=\frac F2\ee^{\iu ka+\kappa a}\left(1-\frac{\iu k}{\kappa}\right).\end{aligned}\]

This produces the hyperbolic representation inside the barrier,

\[\label{eq:barrier_hyperbolic} \psi_{II}(x)=F\ee^{\iu ka}\left[\cosh\kappa(x-a)+\frac{\iu k}{\kappa}\sinh\kappa(x-a)\right].\]

Step 4: Evaluate at \(x=0\). The function and its derivative there are

\[\begin{aligned}A+B&=F\ee^{\iu ka}\left[\cosh(\kappa a)-\frac{\iu k}{\kappa}\sinh(\kappa a)\right],\\ \iu k(A-B)&=F\ee^{\iu ka}\left[-\kappa\sinh(\kappa a)+\iu k\cosh(\kappa a)\right].\end{aligned}\]

Dividing the latter by \(\iu k\) and adding to the former eliminates \(B\):

\[\begin{aligned}2A&=F\ee^{\iu ka}\left[2\cosh(\kappa a)+\iu\left(\frac\kappa k-\frac k\kappa\right)\sinh(\kappa a)\right].\end{aligned}\]

Thus the exact transmission amplitude is

\[\label{eq:tbarrier} \boxed{t=\frac FA=\frac{\ee^{-\iu ka}}{\cosh(\kappa a)+\iu\dfrac{\kappa^2-k^2}{2k\kappa}\sinh(\kappa a)}}.\]

Subtracting the boundary equations similarly gives the reflection amplitude

\[\boxed{r=\frac BA=\frac{-\iu\dfrac{k^2+\kappa^2}{2k\kappa}\sinh(\kappa a)}{\cosh(\kappa a)+\iu\dfrac{\kappa^2-k^2}{2k\kappa}\sinh(\kappa a)}}.\]

Step 5: Calculate flux ratios. Because the potential is zero on both sides, incident and transmitted waves have the same wave number \(k\):

\[j_{\rm inc}=\frac{\hbar k}{m}\abs A^2,\qquad j_{\rm tr}=\frac{\hbar k}{m}\abs F^2,\qquad T=\frac{j_{\rm tr}}{j_{\rm inc}}=\abs t^2.\]

Taking the modulus squared of (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:tbarrier), noting \(\abs{\ee^{-\iu ka}}^2=1\), yields

\[T=\left[\cosh^2(\kappa a)+\frac{(\kappa^2-k^2)^2}{4k^2\kappa^2}\sinh^2(\kappa a)\right]^{-1}.\]

Use \(\cosh^2 y=1+\sinh^2y\) and the identity

\[4k^2\kappa^2+(\kappa^2-k^2)^2=(k^2+\kappa^2)^2\]

to get

\[T=\left[1+\frac{(k^2+\kappa^2)^2}{4k^2\kappa^2}\sinh^2(\kappa a)\right]^{-1}.\]

Since \(k^2+\kappa^2=2mV_0/\hbar^2\) and \(4k^2\kappa^2=16m^2E(V_0-E)/\hbar^4\),

\[\label{eq:Tbarrier} \boxed{T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2}{4E(V_0-E)}\sinh^2\left(\frac{a\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}\hbar\right)\right]^{-1}.}\]

Step 6: Obtain \(R\) and verify conservation. Let \(Q=(k^2+\kappa^2)^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)/(4k^2\kappa^2)\). The reflection amplitude gives \(R=Q/(1+Q)\) while \(T=1/(1+Q)\). Thus \(R+T=1\). Explicitly,

\[\boxed{R=\frac{V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)}{4E(V_0-E)+V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)},\qquad R+T=1.}\]

Even inside the forbidden region, a superposition of exponentials can carry flux:

\[\psi_{II}=C\ee^{\kappa x}+D\ee^{-\kappa x}\implies j_{II}=\frac{2\hbar\kappa}{m}\operatorname{Im}(CD^*)=\frac{\hbar k}{m}\abs F^2.\]

Step 7: Thick-barrier approximation. For \(\kappa a\gg1\), \(\sinh^2(\kappa a)\simeq\ee^{2\kappa a}/4\); the exponential term dominates the denominator in (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:Tbarrier), so

\[\boxed{T\simeq\frac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}\ee^{-2\kappa a} =\frac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}\exp\left(-\frac{2a\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}\right).}\]

Checks. At \(a\to0\), \(T\to1\). At \(a\to\infty\) with \(E<V_0\), \(T\to0\). At \(E\to V_0^-\), use \(\sinh(\kappa a)\sim\kappa a\) to obtain the finite limit \(T\to[1+mV_0a^2/(2\hbar^2)]^{-1}\). The thick-barrier expansion is not valid near this last limit. The exponent is consistent with the WKB action \(2\hbar^{-1}\int_0^a\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}\,\dd x=2\kappa a\).

B5Infinite square well: eigenenergies and normalized eigenfunctions
Question as transcribed in manuscript

Derive (i) the energy eigenvalues and (ii) the normalized eigenfunctions for a particle trapped in an infinite square well of length \(L\).

Detailed worked solution

Let \(V=0\) on \((0,L)\) and \(V=+\infty\) outside. For an ideal infinite well the appropriate boundary conditions are \(\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0\). Inside,

\[\hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2},\qquad-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''=E\psi.\]

Step 1: Prove positivity of nontrivial eigenenergies. Multiply by \(\psi^*\), integrate over \((0,L)\), and integrate by parts:

\[\begin{aligned}E\int_0^L\abs\psi^2\,\dd x &=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_0^L\psi^*\psi''\,\dd x\\ &=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_0^L\abs{\psi'}^2\,\dd x,\end{aligned}\]

where the boundary term \([\psi^*\psi']_0^L\) is zero. For a nonzero function satisfying both Dirichlet conditions, the integral of \(\abs{\psi'}^2\) is positive, hence \(E>0\).

Step 2: Solve the Sturm–Liouville equation. Put \(k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\), so

\[\psi''+k^2\psi=0,\qquad\psi=A\sin kx+B\cos kx.\]

The left condition gives \(B=0\). The right condition gives \(A\sin kL=0\). Since \(A\ne0\), \(\sin kL=0\), i.e. \(kL=n\pi\). Nontrivial states require \(n=1,2,\ldots\), so

\[\boxed{k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L},\qquad E_n=\frac{\hbar^2k_n^2}{2m}=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}=\frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}.}\]

In wavelength language, the wavefunction fits \(n\) half-wavelengths: \(L=n\lambda_n/2\), or \(\lambda_n=2L/n\). The energies are not equally spaced:

\[E_{n+1}-E_n=(2n+1)\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}.\]

Step 3: Normalize. With \(\psi_n=A_n\sin(n\pi x/L)\),

\[\begin{aligned}1&=\abs{A_n}^2\int_0^L\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\dd x =\frac{\abs{A_n}^2}{2}\left[x-\frac{L}{2n\pi}\sin\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right]_0^L =\frac{\abs{A_n}^2L}{2}.\end{aligned}\]

Choosing \(A_n>0\) yields \(A_n=\sqrt{2/L}\), therefore

\[\label{eq:infinite_eigen} \boxed{\psi_n(x)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L),&0<x<L,\\0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad n=1,2,\ldots.}\]

The full stationary state is \(\Psi_n(x,t)=\psi_n(x)\ee^{-\iu E_nt/\hbar}\).

Step 4: Check orthogonality explicitly. For \(m\ne n\), use \(2\sin\alpha\sin\beta=\cos(\alpha-\beta)-\cos(\alpha+\beta)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\langle\psi_m,\psi_n\rangle&=\frac2L\int_0^L\sin\frac{m\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\dd x\\ &=\frac1L\left[\frac{L\sin[(m-n)\pi x/L]}{(m-n)\pi} -\frac{L\sin[(m+n)\pi x/L]}{(m+n)\pi}\right]_0^L=0.\end{aligned}\]

With normalization for \(m=n\) we obtain \(\boxed{\langle\psi_m,\psi_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}}\).

Step 5: Completeness and arbitrary states. The Fourier sine theorem establishes that the normalized eigenfunctions form a complete orthonormal basis of \(L^2(0,L)\). Thus, for any \(f\in L^2(0,L)\),

\[f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\psi_n(x)\quad\text{in }L^2,\qquad c_n=\sqrt{\frac2L}\int_0^L f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\dd x.\]

The distributional resolution of the identity is

\[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi_n(x)\psi_n^*(x')=\delta(x-x'),\qquad 0<x,x'<L.\]

An arbitrary initial normalized state evolves as \(\Psi(x,t)=\sum_nc_n\psi_n(x)\ee^{-\iu E_nt/\hbar}\), with energy outcome probabilities \(\abs{c_n}^2\).

Step 6: Parity. Because the well is centered at \(L/2\), the relevant reflection maps \(x\mapsto L-x\), not \(x\mapsto-x\). By trigonometry,

\[\psi_n(L-x)=\sqrt{2/L}\sin[n\pi-n\pi x/L]=(-1)^{n+1}\psi_n(x).\]

Hence odd \(n\) states are even under reflection about \(L/2\), even \(n\) states are odd, and the \(n\)th wavefunction has \(n-1\) interior nodes.

Step 7: Zero-point energy and rigor. The \(n=0\) sine solution is identically zero. Directly at \(E=0\), the solution \(\psi=Ax+B\) with \(\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0\) also forces \(A=B=0\). Consequently

\[\boxed{E_1=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}>0.}\]

The sharp Poincar\'e inequality \(\int_0^L\abs{\psi'}^2\,\dd x\geq(\pi^2/L^2)\int_0^L\abs\psi^2\,\dd x\) gives the variational bound \(\langle H\rangle\geq E_1\) for any normalized \(\psi\in H_0^1(0,L)\). The self-adjoint Dirichlet Hamiltonian has operator domain \(H^2(0,L)\cap H_0^1(0,L)\); all its spectral eigenvalues are positive, discrete, and simple.

B6Verify \(\Delta x\,\Delta p_x\geq\hbar/2\) in the \(n\)th infinite-well state
Question as transcribed in manuscript

Verify the position–momentum uncertainty relation for the \(n\)th eigenstate of a particle trapped in an infinite square well of length \(L\).

Detailed worked solution

Take the normalized real eigenfunction \(\psi_n=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)\) on \((0,L)\), with \(n\geq1\). Compute

\[(\Delta x)^2=\langle x^2\rangle-\langle x\rangle^2,\qquad (\Delta p)^2=\langle p^2\rangle-\langle p\rangle^2.\]

Step 1: Mean position. Using \(2\sin^2\theta=1-\cos2\theta\) and setting \(\alpha=2n\pi/L\),

\[\begin{aligned}\langle x\rangle&=\frac2L\int_0^Lx\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\dd x\\ &=\frac1L\int_0^Lx\bigl(1-\cos\alpha x\bigr)\,\dd x =\frac1L\left[\frac{L^2}{2}-\left(\frac{x\sin\alpha x}{\alpha}+\frac{\cos\alpha x}{\alpha^2}\right)_0^L\right]\\ &=\boxed{\frac L2},\end{aligned}\]

because \(\sin\alpha L=0\) and \(\cos\alpha L=1\).

Step 2: Mean squared position.

\[\langle x^2\rangle=\frac1L\left[\int_0^Lx^2\,\dd x-\int_0^Lx^2\cos\alpha x\,\dd x\right].\]

The second integral is evaluated by two integrations by parts:

\[\begin{aligned}\int x^2\cos\alpha x\,\dd x&=\frac{x^2\sin\alpha x}{\alpha}-\frac2\alpha\int x\sin\alpha x\,\dd x,\\ \int x\sin\alpha x\,\dd x&=-\frac{x\cos\alpha x}{\alpha}+\frac{\sin\alpha x}{\alpha^2}.\end{aligned}\]

Evaluate at \(0,L\) to obtain

\[\int_0^Lx^2\cos\alpha x\,\dd x=\frac{2L}{\alpha^2}=\frac{L^3}{2n^2\pi^2}.\]

Since \(\int_0^Lx^2\,\dd x=L^3/3\),

\[\boxed{\langle x^2\rangle=L^2\left(\frac13-\frac1{2n^2\pi^2}\right).}\]

Step 3: Position variance. Subtract \(\langle x\rangle^2=L^2/4\):

\[\boxed{(\Delta x)^2=L^2\left(\frac1{12}-\frac1{2n^2\pi^2}\right),\qquad \Delta x=L\sqrt{\frac1{12}-\frac1{2n^2\pi^2}}.}\]

Step 4: Mean momentum. Using \(\hat p=-\iu\hbar\,\dd/\dd x\),

\[\begin{aligned}\langle p\rangle&=-\iu\hbar\int_0^L\psi_n(x)\psi_n'(x)\,\dd x =-\frac{\iu\hbar}{2}\left[\psi_n^2(x)\right]_0^L=\boxed{0}.\end{aligned}\]

Step 5: Second momentum moment. The eigenfunctions obey

\[-\hbar^2\psi_n''(x)=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{L^2}\psi_n(x).\]

The kinetic-energy quadratic form gives

\[\begin{aligned}\langle p^2\rangle&=\hbar^2\int_0^L\abs{\psi_n'}^2\,\dd x =-\hbar^2\int_0^L\psi_n^*\psi_n''\,\dd x\\ &=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{L^2}\int_0^L\abs{\psi_n}^2\,\dd x =\boxed{\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{L^2}}.\end{aligned}\]

The boundary term in the integration by parts vanishes by Dirichlet conditions. Therefore

\[\boxed{\Delta p=\frac{n\pi\hbar}{L}.}\]

Step 6: Uncertainty product. Multiplication gives

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta x\,\Delta p &=\frac{n\pi\hbar}{L}\,L\sqrt{\frac1{12}-\frac1{2n^2\pi^2}}\\ &=\hbar\sqrt{\frac{n^2\pi^2-6}{12}} =\boxed{\frac\hbar2\sqrt{\frac{n^2\pi^2}{3}-2}}.\end{aligned}\]

For \(n\geq1\), \(n^2\pi^2\geq\pi^2>9\), so \((n^2\pi^2/3)-2>1\). Hence

\[\boxed{\Delta x\,\Delta p>\frac\hbar2\quad\text{for every }n\geq1.}\]

Step 7: Ground state and semiclassical limit. At \(n=1\),

\[\Delta x_1\Delta p_1=\hbar\sqrt{\frac{\pi^2-6}{12}}\simeq0.56786\hbar>0.5\hbar.\]

For \(n\to\infty\) at fixed \(L,\hbar\),

\[\Delta x\longrightarrow\frac{L}{\sqrt{12}},\quad \Delta p\sim\frac{n\pi\hbar}{L},\quad \Delta x\Delta p\sim\frac{n\pi\hbar}{\sqrt{12}}.\]

The first limit equals the standard deviation of a uniform classical position distribution on \((0,L)\), consistent with the rapidly oscillating \(\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\) approaching \(1/2\) under coarse graining. The product grows rather than approaching the lower bound.

Operator-domain note. On \(L^2(0,L)\) the Dirichlet differential momentum operator is symmetric but is not itself self-adjoint with that domain. The expression above for \(\langle p^2\rangle\) is rigorously a positive quadratic form. Alternatively extend the wavefunction by zero to \(\R\) and use the self-adjoint full-line momentum operator: the zero extension lies in \(H^1(\R)\), so \(\langle p\rangle\) and \(\|p\psi\|^2\) are meaningful; it need not lie in the domain of the full-line \(p^2\). The usual Heisenberg variance therefore remains well defined. A direct Cauchy–Schwarz verification uses \(u=(x-L/2)\psi_n\) and \(v=-\iu\hbar\psi_n'\); integrating by parts gives \(\langle u,v\rangle=\iu\hbar/2\) and \(\|u\|\|v\|\geq\hbar/2\).

B7Finite symmetric square well: exact bound-state equations and plots
Question as transcribed in manuscript

Solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation for a particle trapped in a finite square well of height \(U\) and obtain the wavefunctions. Plot the first three eigenfunctions and their probability densities. Because the assignment specifies no width or energy convention, we choose the standard symmetric well of depth \(U\) and half-width \(a\), and explicitly state the equivalent positive-height convention.

Detailed worked solution

Step 1: Choose an energy convention. Let

\[\label{eq:Vfinite} \boxed{V(x)=\begin{cases}-U,&\abs x<a,\\0,&\abs x\geq a,\end{cases}\qquad U>0.}\]

The full well width is \(2a\). Bound states have energy \(-U<E<0\). The convention \(V=0\) for \(\abs x<a\) and \(V=U\) outside differs only by the additive constant \(U\) in energy; eigenfunctions and matching equations do not change.

Step 2: Solve in three regions. Introduce

\[k=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E+U)}}\hbar>0,\quad \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{-2mE}}\hbar>0,\quad k^2+\kappa^2=\frac{2mU}{\hbar^2}.\]

The exterior equations \(\psi''-\kappa^2\psi=0\) have only exponentially decaying admissible tails. The interior equation \(\psi''+k^2\psi=0\) has oscillatory solutions. The general square-integrable form is

\[\psi(x)=\begin{cases}L_0\ee^{\kappa(x+a)},&x<-a,\\B\cos(kx)+C\sin(kx),&\abs x\leq a,\\R_0\ee^{-\kappa(x-a)},&x>a.\end{cases}\]

Step 3: Classify states by parity. Since \(V(-x)=V(x)\) and one-dimensional bound eigenvalues are nondegenerate, the eigenfunctions can be chosen even or odd. For an even state, set \(C=0\) and \(L_0=R_0=A_+\cos(ka)\); for an odd state, set \(B=0\), \(L_0=-A_-\sin(ka)\) and \(R_0=A_-\sin(ka)\). Hence

\[\label{eq:finite_even} \boxed{\psi_+(x)=A_+\begin{cases}\cos(ka)\ee^{\kappa(x+a)},&x<-a,\\\cos(kx),&\abs x\leq a,\\\cos(ka)\ee^{-\kappa(x-a)},&x>a,\end{cases}}\]
\[\label{eq:finite_odd} \boxed{\psi_-(x)=A_-\begin{cases}-\sin(ka)\ee^{\kappa(x+a)},&x<-a,\\\sin(kx),&\abs x\leq a,\\\sin(ka)\ee^{-\kappa(x-a)},&x>a.\end{cases}}\]

Step 4: Match at \(x=a\). The boundary-value construction already enforces continuity of \(\psi\); derivative continuity gives

\[\begin{aligned}\text{even:}\quad -A_+k\sin(ka)&=-A_+\kappa\cos(ka)\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{k\tan(ka)=\kappa},\\ \text{odd:}\quad A_-k\cos(ka)&=-A_-\kappa\sin(ka)\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{-k\cot(ka)=\kappa}.\end{aligned}\]

The conditions at \(x=-a\) then follow automatically from parity. The energy eigenvalues cannot generally be written in elementary closed form: they are fixed by roots of these two transcendental equations.

Step 5: Dimensionless quantization. Define the dimensionless variables

\[\boxed{z=ka,\quad q=\kappa a,\quad P=\frac{a\sqrt{2mU}}\hbar.}\]

They satisfy \(z^2+q^2=P^2\), with \(0<z<P\) and \(q=\sqrt{P^2-z^2}\). The allowed roots obey

\[\label{eq:evenodd_finite} \boxed{\begin{aligned}z\tan z&=\sqrt{P^2-z^2}&&\text{(even)},\\ -z\cot z&=\sqrt{P^2-z^2}&&\text{(odd)}. \end{aligned}}\]

For a root \(z_n\), the physical energy is

\[\label{eq:Efinitex} \boxed{E_n=-U+\frac{\hbar^2z_n^2}{2ma^2}=-\frac{\hbar^2q_n^2}{2ma^2}.}\]

With the positive-barrier convention (\(0\) inside, \(U\) outside), the same state has \(E_n'=E_n+U=\hbar^2z_n^2/(2ma^2)\).

Step 6: Normalize the even states. From (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:finite_even), using evenness of \(\abs\psi^2\),

\[\begin{aligned}1&=2\abs{A_+}^2\left[\int_0^a\cos^2(kx)\,\dd x+\cos^2(ka)\int_a^\infty\ee^{-2\kappa(x-a)}\,\dd x\right]\\ &=\abs{A_+}^2\left[a+\frac{\sin(2ka)}{2k}+\frac{\cos^2(ka)}{\kappa}\right].\end{aligned}\]

The even quantization condition gives \(\tan(ka)=\kappa/k\). Thus \(\sin(ka)\cos(ka)=k\kappa/(k^2+\kappa^2)\) and \(\cos^2(ka)=k^2/(k^2+\kappa^2)\), so

\[\frac{\sin(2ka)}{2k}+\frac{\cos^2(ka)}{\kappa} =\frac{\kappa}{k^2+\kappa^2}+\frac{k^2}{\kappa(k^2+\kappa^2)}=\frac1\kappa.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{A_+=\frac1{\sqrt{a+1/\kappa}}.}\]

Step 7: Normalize the odd states. From (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:finite_odd),

\[1=\abs{A_-}^2\left[a-\frac{\sin(2ka)}{2k}+\frac{\sin^2(ka)}{\kappa}\right].\]

The odd condition gives \(\cot(ka)=-\kappa/k\), \(\sin(ka)\cos(ka)=-k\kappa/(k^2+\kappa^2)\), and \(\sin^2(ka)=k^2/(k^2+\kappa^2)\). Hence the bracket is again \(a+1/\kappa\):

\[\boxed{A_-=\frac1{\sqrt{a+1/\kappa}}.}\]

Both parities are normalized by the same formula when evaluated with the \(\kappa\) of the individual state. For each \(n\) let \(N_n=(a+1/\kappa_n)^{-1/2}\); the fully normalized wavefunctions are then exactly (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:finite_even) and (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:finite_odd) with \(A_\pm=N_n\).

Step 8: Count the bound states. A useful single root equation follows by setting \(z=P\sin\theta\), \(q=P\cos\theta\) for \(0<\theta<\pi/2\). Even roots satisfy \(z+\theta=(2j+1)\pi/2\); odd roots satisfy \(z+\theta=(j+1)\pi\). Ordering both families from the ground state with index \(n=0\) yields

\[\label{eq:unifiedf} \boxed{z_n+\arcsin(z_n/P)=\frac{(n+1)\pi}{2},\quad 0<z_n<P,\quad n=0,1,\ldots.}\]

The left side increases strictly on \((0,P)\) and tends to \(P+\pi/2\) at the right endpoint. Thus the \(n\)th bound state exists if and only if \((n+1)\pi/2<P+\pi/2\), or \(P>n\pi/2\). A threshold with \(q=0\) is excluded because the exterior solution would not decay. The exact number of negative-energy bound states is

\[\boxed{N_{\rm bound}=\left\lceil\frac{2P}{\pi}\right\rceil\qquad(P>0).}\]

Three bound states exist exactly when

\[\boxed{P>\pi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad U>\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}.}\]

Any attractive finite square well in one dimension has at least one bound state.

Step 9: Numerically determine and plot the first three states. The assignment supplies no numerical well parameters, so we choose \(P=4\) solely for illustration. This lies in \(\pi<P<3\pi/2\) and supports exactly three bound states. Let \(\varepsilon_0=\hbar^2/(2ma^2)\), so \(U=16\varepsilon_0\). Solve the monotone equation (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:unifiedf) for \(n=0,1,2\). The results are listed in the numerical table below; the roots were independently checked by direct substitution into the parity equations.

Define \(u=x/a\) and \(\varphi_n(u)=\sqrt a\,\psi_n(au)\). The normalized dimensionless amplitudes are \(\sqrt a\,N_n=(1+1/q_n)^{-1/2}\), and \(\int_\R\abs{\varphi_n(u)}^2\,\dd u=1\). Step 10: Find the exterior detection probability. From the normalized even or odd tails, the exact probability outside the geometrical well is

\[\begin{aligned}P_{\rm out}^{(+)}&=2N_n^2\cos^2(k_na)\int_a^\infty \ee^{-2\kappa_n(x-a)}\,\dd x=\frac{N_n^2\cos^2(k_na)}{\kappa_n},\\ P_{\rm out}^{(-)}&=\frac{N_n^2\sin^2(k_na)}{\kappa_n}.\end{aligned}\]

Since \(N_n^2=1/(a+1/\kappa_n)\), these can be written in dimensionless form as \(\cos^2z_n/(q_n+1)\) (even) or \(\sin^2z_n/(q_n+1)\) (odd). For the \(P=4\) example,

\[\boxed{P_{\rm out,0}\simeq2.04\%,\quad P_{\rm out,1}\simeq9.24\%,\quad P_{\rm out,2}\simeq29.34\%.}\]

These probabilities grow with increasing energy toward the continuum threshold in this example. A finite well has a finite bound spectrum, in contrast to the infinite well. The plotted eigenfunctions have respectively zero, one, and two interior nodes.

\[E_n+U\longrightarrow\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots\quad\text{(fixed $n$)}.\]

This reproduces the Dirichlet spectrum of Problem B5 after the appropriate shift in the energy zero.

First edition (1995): Gaussian-weighted polynomial space and Gram--Schmidt

The 1995 Problem 3.27 concerns the finite-dimensional space of Gaussian-weighted polynomials
\[ \Hil_N=\left\{p(x)\ee^{-x^2/2}:\deg p<N\right\},\qquad x\in\R, \]
and asks for verification of its inner-product structure and Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization of the first four natural monomial-Gaussian functions (see manuscript bibliography).

Step 1: Vector space and inner product. For \(f=p\ee^{-x^2/2}\), \(g=q\ee^{-x^2/2}\) and scalars \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb C\),

\[ \alpha f+\beta g=(\alpha p+\beta q)\ee^{-x^2/2}\in\Hil_N. \]
The ordinary \(L^2(\R)\) scalar product is finite since
\[ \langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p^*(x)q(x)\ee^{-x^2}\,\dd x, \]
and every polynomial multiplied by \(\ee^{-x^2}\) is integrable. Conjugate symmetry, complex linearity in the second factor, and positivity follow from the \(L^2\) inner product. A zero norm forces \(p=0\) almost everywhere, hence as a polynomial \(p\equiv0\). Thus \(\Hil_N\) is an \(N\)-dimensional inner-product space and is automatically complete.

Step 2: Gaussian moments. Let \(g_j(x)=x^j\ee^{-x^2/2}\) for \(j=0,1,2,3\). The needed even moments are

\[ \int_\R\ee^{-x^2}\,\dd x=\sqrt\pi,\quad \int_\R x^2\ee^{-x^2}\,\dd x=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2},\quad \int_\R x^4\ee^{-x^2}\,\dd x=\frac{3\sqrt\pi}{4},\quad \int_\R x^6\ee^{-x^2}\,\dd x=\frac{15\sqrt\pi}{8}. \]
All odd Gaussian moments vanish by parity. The formulas follow by differentiating \(I(\alpha)=\int_\R\ee^{-\alpha x^2}\dd x=\sqrt{\pi/\alpha}\) with respect to \(\alpha\).

Step 3: First two orthonormal functions. \(\|g_0\|^2=\sqrt\pi\) gives

\[ \phi_0(x)=\pi^{-1/4}\ee^{-x^2/2}. \]
By odd parity, \(\langle g_0,g_1\rangle=0\). Since \(\|g_1\|^2=\sqrt\pi/2\),
\[ \phi_1(x)=\sqrt2\,\pi^{-1/4}x\ee^{-x^2/2}. \]

Step 4: Third function. The projection of \(g_2\) along \(g_0\) is \((\langle g_0,g_2\rangle/\langle g_0,g_0\rangle)g_0=\frac12g_0\). Thus the orthogonalized vector is

\[ u_2(x)=\left(x^2-\frac12\right)\ee^{-x^2/2}. \]
Its squared norm is \(\int_\R(x^2-\frac12)^2\ee^{-x^2}\dd x=\sqrt\pi/2\) by the moments above, hence
\[ \phi_2(x)=\frac{2x^2-1}{\sqrt2\,\pi^{1/4}}\ee^{-x^2/2}. \]

Step 5: Fourth function. Since \(g_3\) is odd, its projections onto the even \(\phi_0,\phi_2\) vanish. The \(g_1\) projection coefficient is

\[ \frac{\langle g_1,g_3\rangle}{\langle g_1,g_1\rangle} =\frac{3\sqrt\pi/4}{\sqrt\pi/2}=\frac32. \]
Therefore
\[ u_3(x)=\left(x^3-\frac32x\right)\ee^{-x^2/2}. \]
Its squared norm is
\[ \|u_3\|^2=\int_\R(x^6-3x^4+\tfrac94x^2)\ee^{-x^2}\,\dd x =\frac{15\sqrt\pi}{8}-\frac{9\sqrt\pi}{4}+\frac{9\sqrt\pi}{8}=\frac{3\sqrt\pi}{4}. \]
Thus
\[ \phi_3(x)=\frac{2x^3-3x}{\sqrt3\,\pi^{1/4}}\ee^{-x^2/2}. \]

First-edition answer.

\[\begin{aligned} \phi_0(x)&=\pi^{-1/4}\ee^{-x^2/2},\\ \phi_1(x)&=\sqrt2\,\pi^{-1/4}x\ee^{-x^2/2},\\ \phi_2(x)&=\frac{2x^2-1}{\sqrt2\,\pi^{1/4}}\ee^{-x^2/2},\\ \phi_3(x)&=\frac{2x^3-3x}{\sqrt3\,\pi^{1/4}}\ee^{-x^2/2}. \end{aligned}\]

They satisfy \(\langle\phi_j,\phi_k\rangle=\delta_{jk}\). These are the first four normalized Hermite functions: in general \(\phi_n(x)=(\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{2^nn!})^{-1}H_n(x)\ee^{-x^2/2}\). Their appearance connects Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization with the quantum harmonic oscillator.

Third edition (2018): spectral decomposition and exponentials

The third-edition Problem 3.27 asks to express an operator in its orthonormal eigenbasis and show that a spectral definition of an operator function agrees with the operator power series for the exponential (see manuscript bibliography).

Step 1: Complete orthonormal eigenbasis. Suppose \(\hat Q\ket{e_n}=q_n\ket{e_n}\) and \(\langle e_m|e_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\), with completeness \(\sum_n\ket{e_n}\bra{e_n}=\id\). For an arbitrary state \(\ket\alpha\) in the domain,

\[\begin{aligned} \hat Q\ket\alpha &=\hat Q\sum_n\ket{e_n}\braket{e_n}{\alpha} =\sum_nq_n\ket{e_n}\braket{e_n}{\alpha}\\ &=\left(\sum_nq_n\ket{e_n}\bra{e_n}\right)\ket\alpha. \end{aligned}\]
Since the equality holds on the domain for every \(\ket\alpha\),
\[\boxed{\hat Q=\sum_nq_n\ket{e_n}\bra{e_n}.}\]

Step 2: Operator exponential. Since \(\hat Q^j\ket{e_n}=q_n^j\ket{e_n}\) for all nonnegative integers \(j\), the power series gives, first for finite-dimensional or bounded operators,

\[\begin{aligned} \ee^{\hat Q}&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\hat Q^j}{j!} =\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac1{j!}\sum_nq_n^j\ket{e_n}\bra{e_n}\\ &=\sum_n\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q_n^j}{j!}\right)\ket{e_n}\bra{e_n} =\boxed{\sum_n\ee^{q_n}\ket{e_n}\bra{e_n}}. \end{aligned}\]
For unbounded operators, this statement requires the appropriate spectral-functional-calculus definition and domain; formal interchange of all unbounded series is not always justified. A central physical example is the unitary time-evolution operator of a time-independent Hamiltonian,
\[ \boxed{\hat U(t)=\ee^{-\iu\hat Ht/\hbar}=\sum_n\ee^{-\iu E_nt/\hbar}\ket n\bra n} \]
in a complete discrete energy eigenbasis. This shows why each stationary state carries the phase \(\ee^{-\iu E_nt/\hbar}\).
