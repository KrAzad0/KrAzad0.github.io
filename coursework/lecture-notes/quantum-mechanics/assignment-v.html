A1Prove that the Pauli spin matrices are Hermitian and unitary
Original question

Show that the Pauli spin matrices are both Hermitian and unitary.

Detailed worked solution

Definitions and adjoints

In the ordered spin-\(z\) basis \(\{\ket{+z},\ket{-z}\}\), the three Pauli matrices are

\[\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \sigma_y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}. \label{eq:A-paulis}\]

An operator \(A\) is Hermitian if \(A^\dagger=A\), where \(\dagger\) means transpose followed by complex conjugation. Matrix-by-matrix,

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma_x^\dagger&=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=\sigma_x,\\ \sigma_y^\dagger&=\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}^{*}\right)^{\mathsf T} =\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}=\sigma_y,\\ \sigma_z^\dagger&=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}=\sigma_z.\end{aligned}\]

Thus every \(\sigma_i\) is Hermitian. A matrix \(U\) is unitary if \(U^\dagger U=I\). Direct multiplication gives

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma_x^2&=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}^{2}=I_2,\\ \sigma_y^2&=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}^{2} =\begin{pmatrix}(-i)i&0\\0&i(-i)\end{pmatrix}=I_2,\\ \sigma_z^2&=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}^{2}=I_2.\end{aligned}\]

Since \(\sigma_i^\dagger=\sigma_i\), it follows that \(\sigma_i^\dagger\sigma_i=\sigma_i^2=I_2\). Hence

\[\sigma_i^\dagger=\sigma_i,\qquad \sigma_i^\dagger\sigma_i=I_2,\quad i=x,y,z.\]

Physical check.

Hermiticity ensures real spin-component eigenvalues; unitarity of \(\sigma_i\) ensures preservation of the spinor norm under its action. Note that the physical spin operators \(S_i=\hbar\sigma_i/2\) are Hermitian, but are not unitary unless one introduces dimensionless normalization: \(S_i^\dagger S_i=\hbar^2I_2/4\).

Examination conclusion. The Pauli matrices are Hermitian because \(\sigma_i^\dagger=\sigma_i\) and unitary because \(\sigma_i^2=I_2\), so \(\sigma_i^\dagger\sigma_i=I_2\).

A2Prove the Pauli-matrix product identity
Original question

Show that \(\sigma_j\sigma_k=\delta_{jk}I_2+i\sum_l\varepsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l\), where \(j,k,l\in\{x,y,z\}\).

Detailed worked solution

The Pauli matrices in Eq. (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:A-paulis) have squares \(\sigma_x^2=\sigma_y^2=\sigma_z^2=I_2\). This proves the identity for \(j=k\), since \(\delta_{jj}=1\) and \(\varepsilon_{jjl}=0\). For distinct indices, explicitly calculate

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma_x\sigma_y&=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}=i\sigma_z,\\ \sigma_y\sigma_z&=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}=i\sigma_x,\\ \sigma_z\sigma_x&=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}=i\sigma_y.\end{aligned}\]

Reversing the order changes the sign:

\[\sigma_y\sigma_x=-i\sigma_z,\quad \sigma_z\sigma_y=-i\sigma_x,\quad \sigma_x\sigma_z=-i\sigma_y.\]

For distinct \(j,k\), precisely one \(l\) makes \(\varepsilon_{jkl}\) nonzero, and its sign records the order of multiplication. Combining the diagonal and off-diagonal cases yields

\[\sigma_j\sigma_k=\delta_{jk}I_2+i\sum_{l=x,y,z}\varepsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l.\]

Interchange \(j\) and \(k\) and add or subtract the two product identities:

\[\begin{aligned}\{\sigma_j,\sigma_k\}&=\sigma_j\sigma_k+\sigma_k\sigma_j=2\delta_{jk}I_2,\label{eq:A-anti}\\ [\sigma_j,\sigma_k]&=\sigma_j\sigma_k-\sigma_k\sigma_j=2i\sum_l\varepsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l.\label{eq:A-sigcomm}\end{aligned}\]

Conventions.

The identity contains the \(2\times2\) identity \(I_2\) multiplying \(\delta_{jk}\); it is frequently suppressed typographically. Indices \(j,k,l\) label spatial directions, not matrix entries.

Examination conclusion. The explicit cyclic products, reverse products, and squares exhaust all ordered pairs \((j,k)\), establishing the product rule and its commutator/anticommutator corollaries.

A3Verify spin-component and ladder-operator commutators
Original question

Using Pauli spin matrices, verify \([S_x,S_y]=i\hbar S_z\), \([S_z,S_\pm]=\pm\hbar S_\pm\), and \([S_+,S_-]=2\hbar S_z\).

Detailed worked solution

Define

\[S_i=\frac\hbar2\sigma_i,\quad S_\pm=S_x\pm iS_y.\]

Using the Pauli matrices,

\[S_+=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad S_-=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad S_z=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

First identity

Equation (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:A-sigcomm) gives \([\sigma_x,\sigma_y]=2i\sigma_z\). Hence

\[[S_x,S_y]=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}[\sigma_x,\sigma_y] =\frac{i\hbar^2}{2}\sigma_z=i\hbar S_z.\]

Second identity

Direct multiplication yields

\[\begin{aligned}S_zS_+&=\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},& S_+S_z&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\\ S_zS_-&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},& S_-S_z&=\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}\]

Subtracting each pair gives

\[[S_z,S_+]=\hbar S_+,\qquad [S_z,S_-]=-\hbar S_-.\]

Third identity

Similarly,

\[S_+S_-=\hbar^2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad S_-S_+=\hbar^2\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Therefore,

\[[S_+,S_-]=\hbar^2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}=2\hbar S_z.\]
\[[S_x,S_y]=i\hbar S_z,\quad [S_z,S_\pm]=\pm\hbar S_\pm,\quad [S_+,S_-]=2\hbar S_z.\]
Examination conclusion. All three commutators follow exactly from the Pauli algebra and the explicit \(2\times2\) spin-\(\frac12\) matrices.

A4Prove that the spin ladder operators are not Hermitian
Original question

Show that \(\hat S_\pm\) are not Hermitian.

Detailed worked solution

By definition,

\[S_+=S_x+iS_y,\qquad S_-=S_x-iS_y.\]

Since \(S_x^\dagger=S_x\) and \(S_y^\dagger=S_y\), and conjugation changes \(i\) into \(-i\),

\[\begin{aligned}S_+^\dagger&=(S_x+iS_y)^\dagger=S_x-iS_y=S_-,\\ S_-^\dagger&=(S_x-iS_y)^\dagger=S_x+iS_y=S_+.\end{aligned}\]

The operators are unequal. Explicitly,

\[S_+=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad S_+^\dagger=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=S_-\ne S_+.\]

Thus

\[S_+^\dagger=S_-,\qquad S_-^\dagger=S_+,\qquad S_\pm^\dagger\ne S_\pm.\]

Interpretation.

\(S_+\) raises the \(S_z\) magnetic quantum number, whereas \(S_-\) lowers it. They are mutually adjoint rather than individually Hermitian. In contrast, \(S_x=(S_++S_-)/2\) and \(S_y=(S_+-S_-)/(2i)\) are Hermitian observables.

Examination conclusion. Neither \(S_+\) nor \(S_-\) equals its own adjoint; hence neither is Hermitian.

A5Derive momentum as the generator of spatial translations
Original question

Show that momentum is the generator of translations in space.

Detailed worked solution

For an active displacement by \(a\) along \(x\), a wavefunction transforms as

\[(T(a)\psi)(x)=\psi(x-a). \label{eq:A-Tpsi}\]

The minus sign is essential: a packet centered at \(x_0\) is moved to \(x_0+a\). Taylor expansion for infinitesimal \(a\) gives

\[\psi(x-a)=\psi(x)-a\partial_x\psi(x)+O(a^2).\]

In position representation \(p_x=-i\hbar\partial_x\), so \(\partial_x=(i/\hbar)p_x\). Therefore,

\[T(a)=I-\frac{ia}{\hbar}p_x+O(a^2).\]

Composition of translations gives \(T(a)T(b)=T(a+b)\) and, for the strongly continuous unitary family, the finite operator is

\[T(a)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{ia}{\hbar}p_x\right).\]

Its action is confirmed by the translation identity

\[e^{-a\partial_x}\psi(x)=\psi(x-a).\]

For a vector displacement \(\mathbf a\) and many particles, the generator of common translation is the total momentum \(\mathbf P=\sum_a\mathbf p_a\):

\[T(\mathbf a)=\exp[-i\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf P/\hbar].\]

Because momentum is self-adjoint, \(T(a)^\dagger T(a)=I\). The position shift can also be checked using Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff and \([x,p_x]=i\hbar\):

\[\begin{aligned}T(a)^\dagger xT(a)&=e^{ia p_x/\hbar}xe^{-ia p_x/\hbar}=x+a,\\ T(a)^\dagger p_xT(a)&=p_x.\end{aligned}\]

Symmetry consequence.

If \(T(a)^\dagger HT(a)=H\) for every \(a\), differentiation at \(a=0\) gives \([H,p_x]=0\), so \(d p_{x,H}/dt=0\) for a momentum operator with no explicit time dependence.

Examination conclusion. The coefficient of \(-ia/\hbar\) in the infinitesimal unitary translation is \(p_x\), proving that momentum generates translations.

A6Derive the Hamiltonian as the generator of time translations
Original question

Show that the Hamiltonian is the generator of translations in time.

Detailed worked solution

For a closed system with a time-independent self-adjoint Hamiltonian \(H\), the Schr&quot;odinger equation is

\[i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)}=H\ket{\psi(t)}.\]

Define the propagator by \(\ket{\psi(t)}=U(t,t_0)\ket{\psi(t_0)}\), with \(U(t_0,t_0)=I\). It satisfies

\[i\hbar\partial_t U(t,t_0)=H U(t,t_0).\]

For constant \(H\), the solution is

\[U(t,t_0)=e^{-iH(t-t_0)/\hbar}.\]

For a short increment \(\delta t\),

\[U(t_0+\delta t,t_0)=I-\frac{i\delta t}{\hbar}H+O(\delta t^2).\]

Thus \(H\) is the infinitesimal generator of time translations, in exact analogy with the role of momentum for spatial translations. Self-adjointness implies

\[U(t,t_0)^\dagger U(t,t_0)=I,\]

which preserves state norms. On an energy eigenstate \(H\ket E=E\ket E\),

\[U(t,t_0)\ket E=e^{-iE(t-t_0)/\hbar}\ket E,\]

so the energy determines the phase accumulated during time evolution.

Time-dependent qualification.

For \(H(t)\) the short-time generator remains the instantaneous Hamiltonian, but one must generally use

\[U(t,t_0)=\mathcal T\exp\!\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t_0}^t H(s)\,ds\right],\]

where \(\mathcal T\) time-orders noncommuting Hamiltonians. A time-dependent Hamiltonian does not in general give a one-parameter group \(e^{-iH\Delta t/\hbar}\) or energy conservation.

Examination conclusion. The first-order propagator is \(I-iH\delta t/\hbar\), proving that \(H\) generates infinitesimal time evolution.

A7Define the parity operator and determine its eigenvalues
Original question

Define parity operator. Find its eigenvalues.

Detailed worked solution

For a spinless position-space wavefunction on an inversion-invariant domain, parity (spatial inversion) is

\[(\PP\psi)(\mathbf r)=\psi(-\mathbf r).\]

It is a linear operator. Applying it twice,

\[(\PP^2\psi)(\mathbf r)=\psi(-(-\mathbf r))=\psi(\mathbf r),\]

so \(\PP^2=I\). If \(\PP\psi_\pi=\pi\psi_\pi\) for a nonzero eigenfunction, then

\[\psi_\pi=\PP^2\psi_\pi=\pi^2\psi_\pi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi^2=1.\]

Therefore

\[\pi=+1\ \text{(even)},\qquad\pi=-1\ \text{(odd)}.\]

In one dimension, \(\psi_+(-x)=\psi_+(x)\) and \(\psi_-(-x)=-\psi_-(x)\). Every wavefunction decomposes as

\[\psi_\pm(x)=\frac12\left[\psi(x)\pm\psi(-x)\right],\qquad \psi=\psi_++\psi_-,\]

corresponding to orthogonal projectors \(\Pi_\pm=(I\pm\PP)/2\).

Examination conclusion. Parity reverses all spatial coordinates and squares to identity, so its eigenvalues are exactly \(\pm1\).

A8Prove that parity is Hermitian and unitary
Original question

Show that the parity operator is Hermitian and unitary.

Detailed worked solution

For normalized or square-integrable states \(\phi,\psi\), consider the inner product

\[\ip{\PP\phi}{\PP\psi}= \int_{\mathbb R^3}\phi^*(-\mathbf r)\psi(-\mathbf r)\,d^3r.\]

Under \(\mathbf u=-\mathbf r\), the absolute Jacobian is \(1\), and the domain \(\mathbb R^3\) is unchanged. Hence

\[\ip{\PP\phi}{\PP\psi}=\int_{\mathbb R^3}\phi^*(\mathbf u)\psi(\mathbf u)\,d^3u=\ip\phi\psi.\]

Therefore \(\PP^\dagger\PP=I\), proving unitarity and \(\PP^\dagger=\PP^{-1}\). But, by direct action, \(\PP^2=I\), so \(\PP^{-1}=\PP\). Hence

\[\PP^\dagger=\PP^{-1}=\PP,\qquad \PP^\dagger\PP=I.\]

The operator is thus both Hermitian and unitary. Its eigenvalues \(\pm1\) are real and lie on the unit circle, as these properties require.

Scope.

The proof assumes inversion maps the Hilbert space and physical boundary conditions to themselves. A general spinful state may also carry an intrinsic parity factor; this calculation concerns spatial inversion of the orbital wavefunction.

Examination conclusion. Norm preservation establishes unitarity; the involution \(\PP^2=I\) then forces \(\PP^\dagger=\PP\).

A9Differentiate a true scalar from a pseudoscalar
Original question

Differentiate between a true scalar and a pseudoscalar.

Detailed worked solution

Under a proper rotation \(R\in SO(3)\), both a true scalar \(A\) and a pseudoscalar \(B\) are invariant:

\[A'=A,\qquad B'=B\qquad (\det R=+1).\]

The difference appears under an improper orthogonal transformation \(Q\in O(3)\) with \(\det Q=-1\). A true scalar obeys

\[\boxed{A'=A}\qquad\text{for every }Q\in O(3),\]

whereas a pseudoscalar obeys

\[\boxed{B'=(\det Q)B}.\]

In particular, spatial inversion \(Q=-I_3\) has \(\det Q=-1\), so a true scalar is even and a pseudoscalar is odd:

\[A\mapsto A,\qquad B\mapsto-B.\]

Examples.

\(r^2=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r\) and \(\mathbf p^2\) are true scalars. Helicity-type operators \(\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf p\) are pseudoscalars, because \(\mathbf S\) is axial (unchanged by parity) while \(\mathbf p\) is polar (sign reversed). The triple product \(\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)\) of three polar vectors is a pseudoscalar.

A10Differentiate a true vector from a pseudovector
Original question

Differentiate between a true vector and a pseudovector.

Detailed worked solution

Under proper rotations, both polar (true) and axial (pseudo) vectors transform in the same way. If \(R\in SO(3)\),

\[\mathbf V'=R\mathbf V,\qquad\mathbf A'=R\mathbf A.\]

For a general orthogonal transformation \(Q\in O(3)\), the distinction is

\[\boxed{\mathbf V'=Q\mathbf V}\quad\text{(polar vector)},\]
\[\boxed{\mathbf A'=(\det Q)Q\mathbf A}\quad\text{(axial vector)}.\]

For inversion \(Q=-I_3\), \(\det Q=-1\). Consequently,

\[\boxed{\mathbf V\mapsto-\mathbf V,\qquad\mathbf A\mapsto+\mathbf A.}\]

Examples.

Position, momentum, velocity and electric field are polar vectors; orbital angular momentum \(\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p\), spin \(\mathbf S\) and magnetic field are axial vectors. Indeed, under inversion \(\mathbf r\mapsto-\mathbf r\) and \(\mathbf p\mapsto-\mathbf p\), so \(\mathbf L\mapsto(-\mathbf r)\times(-\mathbf p)=\mathbf L\).

A11Show that hydrogenic angular-momentum eigenfunctions have parity \((-1)^l\)
Original question

Show that \(\hat{\mathcal P}\,\psi_{nlm}(r,\theta,\phi)=(-1)^l\psi_{nlm}(r,\theta,\phi)\).

Detailed worked solution

In a central potential the orbital stationary states separate:

\[\psi_{nlm}(r,\theta,\phi)=R_{nl}(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\phi).\]

Under inversion \(\mathbf r\mapsto-\mathbf r\), spherical coordinates transform as

\[r\mapsto r,\qquad\theta\mapsto\pi-\theta,\qquad\phi\mapsto\phi+\pi.\]

The radial factor is unchanged. Write the spherical harmonics schematically as

\[Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)=N_{lm}P_l^m(\cos\theta)e^{im\phi},\]

with the usual convention extended to negative \(m\). The associated Legendre parity identity is

\[P_l^m(-u)=(-1)^{l+m}P_l^m(u),\]

and the azimuthal factor satisfies \(e^{im(\phi+\pi)}=(-1)^m e^{im\phi}\) for integer \(m\). Therefore,

\[\begin{aligned}Y_l^m(\pi-\theta,\phi+\pi) &=(-1)^{l+m}(-1)^mY_l^m(\theta,\phi)\\ &=(-1)^lY_l^m(\theta,\phi).\end{aligned}\]

Consequently,

\[\begin{aligned}(\PP\psi_{nlm})(r,\theta,\phi) &=R_{nl}(r)Y_l^m(\pi-\theta,\phi+\pi)\\ &=(-1)^l\psi_{nlm}(r,\theta,\phi).\end{aligned}\]
\[\PP\psi_{nlm}=(-1)^l\psi_{nlm}.\]

Thus \(s,d,g,\ldots\) orbital states (\(l\) even) have even orbital parity, and \(p,f,\ldots\) (\(l\) odd) have odd orbital parity. The result refers to the orbital spatial factor; intrinsic parity factors must be accounted for separately in a full multi-particle description.

Examination conclusion. Spherical harmonics acquire \((-1)^l\) under inversion while the radial function is unchanged.

A12Derive Laporte's rule from parity
Original question

Derive Laporte's rule.

Detailed worked solution

Let \(H_0\) be an inversion-symmetric unperturbed Hamiltonian, \([H_0,\PP]=0\), and let the initial and final energy eigenstates have definite parity:

\[\PP\ket i=\pi_i\ket i,\qquad\PP\ket f=\pi_f\ket f,\qquad\pi_i,\pi_f=\pm1.\]

The electric dipole operator is \(\mathbf d=\sum_a q_a\mathbf r_a\). Being a polar vector, it is odd under inversion:

\[\PP^\dagger\mathbf d\PP=-\mathbf d.\]

The electric-dipole transition amplitude is proportional to \(\mathbf d_{fi}=\bra f\mathbf d\ket i\). Insert \(I=\PP^\dagger\PP\) on both sides of \(\mathbf d\):

\[\begin{aligned}\mathbf d_{fi} &=\bra f\PP^\dagger(\PP\mathbf d\PP^\dagger)\PP\ket i\\ &=\pi_f\pi_i(-1)\bra f\mathbf d\ket i\\ &=-\pi_f\pi_i\mathbf d_{fi}.\end{aligned}\]

Thus \((1+\pi_f\pi_i)\mathbf d_{fi}=0\). For a nonzero electric-dipole matrix element,

\[\pi_f\pi_i=-1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\pi_f=-\pi_i.\]

This is Laporte's rule: for a centrosymmetric system, electric-dipole transitions are forbidden between states of the same parity (\(g\leftrightarrow g\) or \(u\leftrightarrow u\)), and parity allows \(g\leftrightarrow u\) transitions. The labels \(g\) (gerade) and \(u\) (ungerade) refer to even and odd inversion parity. For a one-electron central potential \(\pi=(-1)^l\), so parity requires \(\Delta l\) odd; the angular momentum selection rules for an E1 photon further restrict the familiar single-electron case to \(\Delta l=\pm1\) (with suitable magnetic quantum number conditions).

Limitation.

Opposite parity is a necessary, not sufficient, condition for a nonzero E1 amplitude. Magnetic-dipole or electric-quadrupole transitions, external parity breaking, or state mixing can permit processes forbidden in the pure E1 inversion-symmetric approximation.

Examination conclusion. Because the dipole operator is odd, its matrix element between equal-parity states vanishes; the E1 transition must change parity.

A13Show that hydrogen's internal ground state has zero mean electron momentum
Original question

Show that if the electron in the hydrogen atom is in the ground state, then necessarily \(\avg{\mathbf p_e}=\mathbf0\).

Detailed worked solution

We take the standard nucleus-fixed (or internal relative-coordinate) hydrogen Hamiltonian and its normalized real \(1s\) wavefunction,

\[\psi_{100}(\mathbf r)=\frac1{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}}e^{-r/a_0},\quad r=|\mathbf r|,\]

where \(a_0\) is the Bohr radius for the chosen reduced-mass convention. The momentum operator is \(\mathbf p=-i\hbar\grad\). Its mean is

\[\avg{\mathbf p}=\int_{\mathbb R^3}\psi_{100}^*(\mathbf r)(-i\hbar\grad)\psi_{100}(\mathbf r)\,d^3r.\]

Because \(\psi_{100}\) depends only on \(r\),

\[\grad\psi_{100}=-\frac1{a_0}\psi_{100}\,\hat{\mathbf r}.\]

Thus

\[\avg{\mathbf p}=\frac{i\hbar}{a_0}\int |\psi_{100}(r)|^2\hat{\mathbf r}\,d^3r.\]

The angular integral of the radial unit vector vanishes by spherical symmetry,

\[\int_{S^2}\hat{\mathbf r}\,d\Omega=\mathbf0,\]

so

\[\avg{\mathbf p_e}=\mathbf0\qquad\text{(hydrogen internal ground state / nucleus-fixed frame)}.\]

An independent symmetry proof uses parity. The \(1s\) orbital has \(l=0\), hence \(\PP\ket{1s}=+\ket{1s}\), whereas \(\PP\mathbf p\PP=-\mathbf p\). Therefore

\[\avg{\mathbf p}=\bra{1s}\PP\mathbf p\PP\ket{1s}=-\avg{\mathbf p},\]

which forces \(\avg{\mathbf p}=0\).

Physical qualification.

The phrase "electron momentum" refers here to internal/relative motion in the atomic rest frame or the conventional fixed-nucleus approximation. A freely moving hydrogen atom can have a nonzero center-of-mass momentum, so the laboratory electron momentum need not vanish merely because the atom is internally in its ground state.

Examination conclusion. The normalized \(1s\) state is spherically symmetric and even under parity, while momentum is parity odd; its expectation value therefore vanishes.

A14Show that \(L_z\) generates rotations about the \(z\)-axis
Original question

Show that \(\hat L_z\) is the generator of rotations about the \(z\)-axis.

Detailed worked solution

In Cartesian coordinates the orbital angular momentum is

\[L_z=xp_y-yp_x=-i\hbar\left(x\partial_y-y\partial_x\right).\]

Use \(x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi\), \(y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi\), so

\[\partial_\phi=-y\partial_x+x\partial_y.\]

Hence

\[L_z=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}.\]

An active rotation by angle \(\alpha\) about the \(z\) axis carries the wavefunction according to

\[(U_z(\alpha)\psi)(r,\theta,\phi)=\psi(r,\theta,\phi-\alpha).\]

For small \(\alpha\), Taylor expansion gives

\[\psi(\phi-\alpha)=\psi(\phi)-\alpha\partial_\phi\psi(\phi)+O(\alpha^2).\]

Substitute \(\partial_\phi=(i/\hbar)L_z\):

\[U_z(\alpha)=I-\frac{i\alpha}{\hbar}L_z+O(\alpha^2).\]

Finite rotations follow by exponentiation:

\[U_z(\alpha)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{i\alpha}{\hbar}L_z\right).\]

The Heisenberg action confirms the geometric rotation. Since \([L_z,x]=i\hbar y\) and \([L_z,y]=-i\hbar x\),

\[\begin{aligned}U_z^\dagger(\alpha)xU_z(\alpha)&=x\cos\alpha-y\sin\alpha,\\ U_z^\dagger(\alpha)yU_z(\alpha)&=x\sin\alpha+y\cos\alpha.\end{aligned}\]

For eigenfunctions \(e^{im\phi}\), \(L_ze^{im\phi}=m\hbar e^{im\phi}\), and a rotation multiplies the state by \(e^{-im\alpha}\).

Examination conclusion. The coefficient of \(-i\alpha/\hbar\) in the infinitesimal \(z\)-rotation operator is \(L_z\), establishing it as the generator.

A15Explain the connection between symmetry and degeneracy
Original question

Explain the connection between symmetry and degeneracy in brief.

Detailed worked solution

Let \(U(g)\) be a unitary symmetry of a Hamiltonian \(H\), so

\[U(g)^\dagger HU(g)=H\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[H,U(g)]=0.\]

Suppose \(H\ket{E,\alpha}=E\ket{E,\alpha}\). Then

\[H\bigl(U(g)\ket{E,\alpha}\bigr) =U(g)H\ket{E,\alpha} =E\bigl(U(g)\ket{E,\alpha}\bigr).\]

Therefore every symmetry operation maps an energy eigenstate into the same energy eigenspace. If the transformed state is linearly independent of the original state, a degeneracy follows. If it is merely proportional, no degeneracy is forced. For full rotational invariance,

\[[H,J_x]=[H,J_y]=[H,J_z]=0,\]

so \([H,J_\pm]=0\). The ladder formula

\[J_\pm\ket{j,m}=\hbar\sqrt{(j\mp m)(j\pm m+1)}\ket{j,m\pm1}\]

connects states of the same energy with \(m=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j\). One irreducible multiplet therefore has

\[g_j=2j+1.\]

Caveats.

A symmetry does not automatically generate degeneracy: parity has only one-dimensional irreducible representations, and the inversion-symmetric one-dimensional harmonic oscillator has nondegenerate bound-state levels. Similarly, \(j=0\) is a rotational singlet. Further "accidental" degeneracies can arise from extra dynamical symmetries, such as the Runge–Lenz symmetry of the Coulomb problem; an external field can break full rotational symmetry and split a multiplet.

Examination conclusion. A symmetry preserves energy eigenspaces. Degeneracy is enforced when a multidimensional irreducible representation of the symmetry acts within an energy eigenspace, as with the \(2j+1\) magnetic states of a rotation multiplet.

Original question

Construct the spin matrices for an electron using the eigenbasis of \(\hat S^2\) and \(\hat S_z\).

Detailed worked solution

Basis and ladder algebra

For an electron \(s=\tfrac12\), with the ordered orthonormal basis

\[\ket+\equiv\ket{\tfrac12,\tfrac12}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad \ket-\equiv\ket{\tfrac12,-\tfrac12}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},\]

we have \(\hat S^2\ket{s,m}=\hbar^2s(s+1)\ket{s,m}\) and \(\hat S_z\ket{s,m}=\hbar m\ket{s,m}\). Hence

\[\hat S^2=\frac{3\hbar^2}{4}I_2,\qquad \hat S_z=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

\[\hat S_\pm\ket{s,m}=\hbar\sqrt{s(s+1)-m(m\pm1)}\ket{s,m\pm1}.\]

For \(s=\frac12\), the coefficient is \(\sqrt{3/4-(-1/4)}=1\) on the nonzero steps, so

\[\hat S_+\ket-=\hbar\ket+ ,\quad \hat S_+\ket+=0,\quad \hat S_-\ket+=\hbar\ket-,\quad \hat S_-\ket-=0.\]

Reading off columns yields

\[\hat S_+=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \hat S_-=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.\]

Since \(\hat S_x=(\hat S_++\hat S_-)/2\) and \(\hat S_y=(\hat S_+-\hat S_-)/(2i)\), we obtain

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned} \hat S_x&=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},& \hat S_y&=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},& \hat S_z&=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}} \label{eq:spinmats}\]

\[[\hat S_x,\hat S_y]=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}[\sigma_x,\sigma_y] =\frac{\hbar^2}{4}(2i\sigma_z)=i\hbar\hat S_z,\]

with cyclic analogues. Each \(\sigma_i^2=I_2\), so

\[\hat S^2=\sum_i\hat S_i^2=3\frac{\hbar^2}{4}I_2,\]

consistent with \(s(s+1)\hbar^2=3\hbar^2/4\).

Examination conclusion. In the \(\ket{s=\frac12,m=\pm\frac12}\) basis, the spin matrices are exactly \(\hat S_i=\hbar\sigma_i/2\); the ladder construction fixes all off-diagonal entries, and the commutator and Casimir checks validate the result.

B3Eigenvalues and normalized eigenvectors of the Pauli matrices
Original question

Determine the eigenvalues and the normalized eigenvectors of the Pauli spin matrices.

Detailed worked solution

All Pauli matrices are Hermitian and satisfy \(\sigma_i^2=I_2\). The eigenvalue problem is \(\sigma_i v=\lambda v\). We explicitly evaluate each characteristic polynomial and solve the linear systems.

Matrix \(\sigma_x\)

\[\det(\sigma_x-\lambda I)=\begin{vmatrix}-\lambda&1\\1&-\lambda\end{vmatrix}=\lambda^2-1.\]

Thus \(\lambda=\pm1\). At \(\lambda=+1\), the system \(-a+b=0\), \(a-b=0\) gives \(b=a\); norm \(2|a|^2=1\) permits \(a=1/\sqrt2\). At \(\lambda=-1\), \(a+b=0\) gives \(b=-a\). Therefore

\[\boxed{\ket{+x}=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \ket{-x}=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.}\]

Matrix \(\sigma_y\)

\[\det(\sigma_y-\lambda I)=\begin{vmatrix}-\lambda&-i\\i&-\lambda\end{vmatrix} =\lambda^2-(-i)i=\lambda^2-1.\]

For \(\lambda=+1\), \(-a-ib=0\), i.e. \(b=ia\). For \(\lambda=-1\), \(a-ib=0\), i.e. \(b=-ia\). Normalizing gives

\[\boxed{\ket{+y}=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix},\qquad \ket{-y}=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}.}\]

Matrix \(\sigma_z\)

\[\det(\sigma_z-\lambda I)=\begin{vmatrix}1-\lambda&0\\0&-1-\lambda\end{vmatrix} =\lambda^2-1.\]

For \(\lambda=+1\), the second component vanishes; for \(\lambda=-1\), the first vanishes:

\[\boxed{\ket{+z}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \ket{-z}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.}\]

Orthogonality, projectors, completeness, and phases

For each \(i=x,y,z\), verify \(\ip{+i}{+i}=\ip{-i}{-i}=1\) and \(\ip{+i}{-i}=0\). For example, \(\ip{+y}{-y}=\frac12(1+(-i)(-i))=0\). Let \(\Pi_{i,\pm}=\ket{\pm i}\bra{\pm i}\). Direct multiplication gives

\[\begin{aligned}\Pi_{x,\pm}&=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&\pm1\\\pm1&1\end{pmatrix},& \Pi_{y,\pm}&=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&\mp i\\\pm i&1\end{pmatrix},\\ \Pi_{z,+}&=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},& \Pi_{z,-}&=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}\]

Thus \(\Pi_{i,+}+\Pi_{i,-}=I_2\), \(\Pi_{i,+}\Pi_{i,-}=0\), and \(\Pi_{i,\pm}^2=\Pi_{i,\pm}=\Pi_{i,\pm}^\dagger\). The spectral decompositions are

\[\boxed{\sigma_i=\Pi_{i,+}-\Pi_{i,-},\qquad \Pi_{i,\pm}=\frac{I_2\pm\sigma_i}{2}.}\]

Multiplying any eigenvector by \(e^{i\chi}\) preserves its norm, eigenvalue and projector: this is the physically irrelevant global phase freedom of a single eigenket. The eigenvalues of \(\hat S_i=\hbar\sigma_i/2\) are \(\pm\hbar/2\).

Examination conclusion. Each Pauli matrix has characteristic polynomial \(\lambda^2-1\) and eigenvalues \(\pm1\). The above six kets are normalized orthogonal eigenbases; their projectors sum to the identity and yield the spectral resolutions \(\sigma_i=\Pi_{i,+}-\Pi_{i,-}\).

Original question

For \(\ket\psi=(a,b)^{\mathsf T}\), express the electron spin state as a linear combination of eigenvectors of (i) \(\hat S_z\), (ii) \(\hat S_x\), and (iii) \(\hat S_y\).

Detailed worked solution

Assume \(a,b\in\mathbb C\) and \(|a|^2+|b|^2=1\). For an orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_+},\ket{u_-}\}\), completeness is \(I=\ket{u_+}\bra{u_+}+\ket{u_-}\bra{u_-}\). Hence

\[\ket\psi=\ip{u_+}{\psi}\ket{u_+}+\ip{u_-}{\psi}\ket{u_-}.\]

The coefficients are inner-product projection amplitudes, and their squared moduli are measurement probabilities.

\(S_z\) basis

Using \(\bra{+z}=(1,0)\) and \(\bra{-z}=(0,1)\),

\[\boxed{\ket\psi=a\ket{+z}+b\ket{-z}.}\]

Reconstruction: \(a(1,0)^{\mathsf T}+b(0,1)^{\mathsf T}=(a,b)^{\mathsf T}\). The probabilities are \(P(z,+)=|a|^2\) and \(P(z,-)=|b|^2\).

\(S_x\) basis

Projection gives

\[\begin{aligned}c_{x,+}&=\ip{+x}{\psi}=\frac{a+b}{\sqrt2},& c_{x,-}&=\ip{-x}{\psi}=\frac{a-b}{\sqrt2}.\end{aligned}\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{\ket\psi=\frac{a+b}{\sqrt2}\ket{+x}+\frac{a-b}{\sqrt2}\ket{-x}.}\]

To reconstruct explicitly,

\[\frac12\left[(a+b)\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}+(a-b)\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\right] =\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}.\]

The probabilities are

\[\boxed{P(x,\pm)=\frac{|a\pm b|^2}{2};\quad P(x,+)+P(x,-)=|a|^2+|b|^2=1.}\]

\(S_y\) basis

Conjugating the eigenkets is essential: \(\bra{+y}=(1,-i)/\sqrt2\), \(\bra{-y}=(1,i)/\sqrt2\). Thus

\[\begin{aligned}c_{y,+}&=\ip{+y}{\psi}=\frac{a-ib}{\sqrt2},& c_{y,-}&=\ip{-y}{\psi}=\frac{a+ib}{\sqrt2}.\end{aligned}\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{\ket\psi=\frac{a-ib}{\sqrt2}\ket{+y}+\frac{a+ib}{\sqrt2}\ket{-y}.}\]

Reconstructing,

\[\frac12\left[(a-ib)\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}+(a+ib)\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}\right] =\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}.\]

The probabilities are \(P(y,+)=|a-ib|^2/2\) and \(P(y,-)=|a+ib|^2/2\), whose sum is one.

Unitary basis changes

The matrices whose columns are the eigenvectors are

\[U_x=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad U_y=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\i&-i\end{pmatrix}.\]

Both satisfy \(U_i^\dagger U_i=I_2\). With \([\psi]_z=(a,b)^{\mathsf T}\), the coefficient columns are \([\psi]_x=U_x^\dagger[\psi]_z\) and \([\psi]_y=U_y^\dagger[\psi]_z\). Unitarity preserves the norm, proving the probability identities without separate expansion.

Examination conclusion. All three expansions follow from \(c_{i,\pm}=\ip{\pm i}{\psi}\). Their reconstruction and probability normalization confirm that each basis describes the identical state, not three different physical preparations.

B6Translational invariance and conservation of momentum
Original question

Show that momentum is conserved if the Hamiltonian of a system is invariant under spatial translations.

Detailed worked solution

Momentum generates translations

In one dimension a positive physical displacement \(a\) acts on a wavefunction according to \((\UU(a)\psi)(x)=\psi(x-a)\). For infinitesimal \(a\), Taylor's theorem gives

\[\psi(x-a)=\psi(x)-a\partial_x\psi(x)+O(a^2) =\left(I-\frac{ia}{\hbar}\hat p\right)\psi(x)+O(a^2),\]

because \(\hat p=-i\hbar\partial_x\). Composition of infinitesimal displacements produces the unitary finite translation operator

\[\boxed{\UU(a)=e^{-ia\hat p/\hbar},\qquad \UU^\dagger(a)=\UU(-a).}\]

For a simultaneous displacement of an isolated \(N\)-particle system, the generator is total momentum \(\hat{\mathbf P}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^N\hat{\mathbf p}_\alpha\), giving \(\UU(\mathbf a)=e^{-i\mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf P}/\hbar}\). Translation by the same \(\mathbf a\) leaves all relative separations invariant.

Infinitesimal symmetry proof

Translation invariance along \(x\) means

\[\UU^\dagger(a)\hat H\UU(a)=\hat H\qquad\text{for every real }a. \label{eq:translation-symmetry}\]

Expand \(\UU(a)=I-ia\hat P_x/\hbar+O(a^2)\) and its adjoint. Then

\[\begin{aligned}\UU^\dagger\hat H\UU &=\left(I+\frac{ia}{\hbar}\hat P_x\right)\hat H \left(I-\frac{ia}{\hbar}\hat P_x\right)+O(a^2)\\ &=\hat H+\frac{ia}{\hbar}(\hat P_x\hat H-\hat H\hat P_x)+O(a^2).\end{aligned}\]

Because Eq. (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:translation-symmetry) holds at every \(a\), its coefficient linear in \(a\) must vanish:

\[\boxed{[\hat H,\hat P_x]=0.}\]

Repeat along \(y\) and \(z\). Full spatial translation invariance yields

\[\boxed{[\hat H,\hat P_i]=0\ (i=x,y,z).}\]

Heisenberg equation and Ehrenfest theorem

For an observable without explicit time dependence,

\[\frac{d\hat A_H}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat H_H,\hat A_H].\]

With \(\hat A=\hat P_i\), the vanishing commutator gives

\[\boxed{\frac{d\hat P_{i,H}}{dt}=0,\qquad\frac{d}{dt}\avg{\hat P_i}=0.}\]

This holds for every state in the physical domain, whether or not the state is a momentum eigenstate. The entire momentum spectral distribution is invariant when the symmetry commutes with time evolution.

Explicit force-law check

For \(\hat H=\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}/(2m)+V(\hat{\mathbf r})\), on smooth test wavefunctions

\[\begin{aligned}[V,\hat p_x]\psi &=V(-i\hbar\partial_x\psi)+i\hbar\partial_x(V\psi) =i\hbar(\partial_xV)\psi.\end{aligned}\]

Thus \([\hat H,\hat p_i]=i\hbar\partial_iV\) and

\[\boxed{\frac{d\hat p_{i,H}}{dt}=-(\partial_i V)_H,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\avg{\hat p_i}=-\avg{\partial_iV}.}\]

For full translation invariance, \(V(\mathbf r+\mathbf a)=V(\mathbf r)\) for arbitrary \(\mathbf a\), hence \(\grad V=0\) for a differentiable potential. For translation invariance only along \(x\), merely \(\partial_xV=0\) is needed to conserve \(p_x\). In an interacting system, relative potentials can be nonconstant even though total momentum remains conserved; one must then use \(\hat{\mathbf P}\), not any individual \(\hat{\mathbf p}_\alpha\).

Examination conclusion. Continuous translation invariance implies \([\hat H,\hat P_i]=0\) by differentiation of \(\UU^\dagger\hat H\UU=\hat H\) at the identity; the Heisenberg equation then proves \(d\hat P_{i,H}/dt=0\). This is the quantum translation–momentum conservation statement associated with Noether's theorem.

B7Two particles coupled by a quadratic relative potential
Original question

For particles of masses \(m_1,m_2\) in one dimension with \(\hat H=\hat p_1^2/(2m_1)+\hat p_2^2/(2m_2)+\frac k2(\hat x_1-\hat x_2)^2\), prove that \(\hat P=\hat p_1+\hat p_2\) is conserved.

Detailed worked solution

Direct canonical commutator proof

Assume \([\hat x_i,\hat p_j]=i\hbar\delta_{ij}\), \([\hat x_i,\hat x_j]=[\hat p_i,\hat p_j]=0\). Set \(\hat r=\hat x_1-\hat x_2\) and \(\hat P=\hat p_1+\hat p_2\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}[\hat r,\hat p_1]&=i\hbar,& [\hat r,\hat p_2]&=-i\hbar,\\ [\hat r,\hat P]&=[\hat r,\hat p_1]+[\hat r,\hat p_2]=0.\end{aligned}\]

The product rule \([A^2,B]=A[A,B]+[A,B]A\) yields \([\hat r^2,\hat P]=0\). Since all momenta commute,

\[\left[\frac{\hat p_1^2}{2m_1}+\frac{\hat p_2^2}{2m_2},\hat P\right]=0.\]

Consequently,

\[\boxed{[\hat H,\hat P]=\frac{k}{2}[\hat r^2,\hat P]=0,\qquad \frac{d\hat P_H}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat H_H,\hat P_H]=0.} \label{eq:2particle-conserve}\]

Internal forces

In the Heisenberg picture,

\[\begin{aligned}\dot{\hat p}_{1,H}&=\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat H_H,\hat p_{1,H}]=-k(\hat x_{1,H}-\hat x_{2,H}),\\ \dot{\hat p}_{2,H}&=\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat H_H,\hat p_{2,H}]=+k(\hat x_{1,H}-\hat x_{2,H}).\end{aligned}\]

Here the interaction commutators are \([k\hat r^2/2,\hat p_1]=i\hbar k\hat r\) and \([k\hat r^2/2,\hat p_2]=-i\hbar k\hat r\). The internal forces cancel exactly, \(\dot{\hat p}_{1,H}+\dot{\hat p}_{2,H}=0\). Individual momenta vary, while their sum is conserved.

Canonical center-of-mass transformation

Define total and reduced masses

\[M=m_1+m_2,\qquad \mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{M},\qquad \frac1\mu=\frac1{m_1}+\frac1{m_2},\]

and operators

\[\begin{aligned}\hat R&=\frac{m_1\hat x_1+m_2\hat x_2}{M}, & \hat r&=\hat x_1-\hat x_2,\\ \hat P&=\hat p_1+\hat p_2, & \hat p&=\frac{m_2\hat p_1-m_1\hat p_2}{M}. \label{eq:com}\end{aligned}\]

Check each canonical commutator directly:

\[\begin{aligned}[\hat R,\hat P] &=\frac{m_1}{M}[\hat x_1,\hat p_1]+\frac{m_2}{M}[\hat x_2,\hat p_2] =i\hbar,\\ [\hat r,\hat p] &=\frac{m_2}{M}[\hat x_1,\hat p_1]+\frac{m_1}{M}[\hat x_2,\hat p_2] =i\hbar,\\ [\hat R,\hat p] &=\frac{m_1m_2}{M^2}[\hat x_1,\hat p_1] -\frac{m_1m_2}{M^2}[\hat x_2,\hat p_2]=0,\\ [\hat r,\hat P]&=i\hbar-i\hbar=0.\end{aligned}\]

The remaining commutators \([\hat R,\hat r]\) and \([\hat P,\hat p]\) vanish because all original positions commute, and all original momenta commute. Thus \((\hat R,\hat P)\) and \((\hat r,\hat p)\) are independent canonical pairs.

Inverse transformation and kinetic energy

Solving Eq. (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:com) yields

\[\begin{aligned}\hat x_1&=\hat R+\frac{m_2}{M}\hat r,&\hat x_2&=\hat R-\frac{m_1}{M}\hat r,\\ \hat p_1&=\frac{m_1}{M}\hat P+\hat p,&\hat p_2&=\frac{m_2}{M}\hat P-\hat p.\end{aligned}\]

Substitute into the kinetic energy. Since \([\hat P,\hat p]=0\), we may expand normally:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\hat p_1^2}{2m_1}&=\frac{m_1}{2M^2}\hat P^2+\frac1M\hat P\hat p+\frac{\hat p^2}{2m_1},\\ \frac{\hat p_2^2}{2m_2}&=\frac{m_2}{2M^2}\hat P^2-\frac1M\hat P\hat p+\frac{\hat p^2}{2m_2}.\end{aligned}\]

The mixed terms cancel, and we obtain

\[\boxed{\hat H=\underbrace{\frac{\hat P^2}{2M}}_{\hat H_{\rm CM}} +\underbrace{\left(\frac{\hat p^2}{2\mu}+\frac{k}{2}\hat r^2\right)}_{\hat H_{\rm rel}}.} \label{eq:separate}\]

Because \([\hat P,\hat r]=[\hat P,\hat p]=0\), the center-of-mass and relative Hamiltonians commute. The center-of-mass coordinate obeys \(\dot{\hat R}_H=\hat P_H/M\), \(\ddot{\hat R}_H=0\). The relative equations are \(\dot{\hat r}_H=\hat p_H/\mu\), \(\dot{\hat p}_H=-k\hat r_H\), so

\[\ddot{\hat r}_H+\omega^2\hat r_H=0,\qquad \omega=\sqrt{k/\mu}\quad(k>0).\]

For \(k>0\), the relative subsystem is a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator, with \(E_{{\rm rel},n}=\hbar\omega(n+\frac12)\). The total generalized stationary energies are

\[\boxed{E_{P,n}=\frac{P^2}{2M}+\hbar\sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}}\left(n+\frac12\right).}\]

On the whole line, a definite-\(P\) plane wave is delta-normalized rather than square-integrable; normalized wavepackets can be used for the center-of-mass sector. The linear coordinate transformation has Jacobian determinant \(-1\), so \(dx_1\,dx_2=dR\,dr\) and no extra factor appears in the norm. The frequency result assumes a stable \(k>0\) oscillator, whereas the conservation proof is valid for any real \(k\).

Examination conclusion. The total momentum \(\hat P\) commutes with the Hamiltonian because the relative coordinate commutes with \(\hat P\). The center-of-mass transformation proves this independently and splits the problem into a free center of mass and a harmonic internal mode.

B8Inversion symmetry and parity consequences
Original question

Explain inversion symmetry of a Hamiltonian and its consequences.

Detailed worked solution

Definition, parity square, and self-adjointness

Spatial inversion is \(\mathbf r\mapsto-\mathbf r\). The unitary parity operator \(\PP\) is defined on position wavefunctions by

\[\boxed{(\PP\psi)(\mathbf r)=\psi(-\mathbf r).}\]

Twice applied, \((\PP^2\psi)(\mathbf r)=\psi(\mathbf r)\); therefore \(\PP^2=I\) and \(\PP^{-1}=\PP\). It preserves inner products:

\[\ip{\PP\phi}{\PP\psi}=\int d^3r\,\phi^*(-\mathbf r)\psi(-\mathbf r) =\int d^3u\,\phi^*(\mathbf u)\psi(\mathbf u)=\ip\phi\psi.\]

Thus \(\PP^\dagger\PP=I\), and together with \(\PP^{-1}=\PP\),

\[\boxed{\PP^2=I,\qquad \PP^\dagger=\PP^{-1}=\PP.}\]

For a nonzero parity eigenstate \(\PP\ket\psi=\pi\ket\psi\), we have \(\pi^2=1\); hence \(\pi=\pm1\). Even and odd wavefunctions satisfy \(\psi_+(-\mathbf r)=\psi_+(\mathbf r)\), \(\psi_-(-\mathbf r)=-\psi_-(\mathbf r)\), respectively.

Transformation of observables and Hamiltonian

Applying \(\PP\) on either side of position and momentum yields

\[\boxed{\PP\hat{\mathbf r}\PP=-\hat{\mathbf r},\qquad \PP\hat{\mathbf p}\PP=-\hat{\mathbf p}.}\]

For example, in one dimension, \((\PP\hat x\PP\psi)(x)=-x\psi(x)\). For momentum, the chain rule gives \(-i\hbar\partial_x\psi(-x)=+i\hbar\psi'(-x)\), and the outer parity then yields \(-\hat p\psi(x)\). Squared momentum is even, while \(\hat{\mathbf L}=\hat{\mathbf r}\times\hat{\mathbf p}\) is axial and unchanged under parity. Spin is also axial under ordinary inversion.

For \(\hat H=\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}/(2m)+V(\hat{\mathbf r})\), assuming an inversion-symmetric operator domain,

\[\PP\hat H\PP=\frac{\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}}{2m}+V(-\hat{\mathbf r}).\]

Hence the sufficient and, for this model, necessary symmetry criterion is \(V(-\mathbf r)=V(\mathbf r)\). Under this condition

\[\boxed{\PP^\dagger\hat H\PP=\hat H\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[\hat H,\PP]=0.} \label{eq:parity}\]

Conservation and simultaneous eigenstates

If \([\hat H,\PP]=0\) and \(\PP\) has no explicit time dependence, then \(d\PP_H/dt=(i/\hbar)[\hat H_H,\PP_H]=0\). Thus the probabilities for even and odd parity are conserved. Furthermore, if \(\hat H\ket E=E\ket E\), then \(\hat H(\PP\ket E)=E(\PP\ket E)\). If the energy level is nondegenerate, \(\PP\ket E=\pm\ket E\). In a degenerate eigenspace, one may choose an orthonormal simultaneous eigenbasis of \(\hat H\) and \(\PP\), although arbitrary superpositions of opposite parity at the same energy need not have definite parity.

Parity projectors and selection rule

The spectral projectors are

\[\boxed{\Pi_\pm=\frac{I\pm\PP}{2},\qquad\Pi_\pm^2=\Pi_\pm,\quad\Pi_+\Pi_-=0,\quad\Pi_++\Pi_-=I.}\]

For a state \(\psi\), they produce \(\psi_\pm(\mathbf r)=[\psi(\mathbf r)\pm\psi(-\mathbf r)]/2\). If an operator \(\hat O\) has parity \(\eta_O\), meaning \(\PP\hat O\PP=\eta_O\hat O\), and initial and final eigenstates have parity \(\pi_i,\pi_f\), then

\[\ip{f}{\hat O\ket i}=\pi_f\pi_i\eta_O\,\ip{f}{\hat O\ket i},\]

and therefore

\[\boxed{(1-\pi_f\pi_i\eta_O)\,\bra f\hat O\ket i=0.}\]

A nonzero matrix element requires \(\pi_f\pi_i\eta_O=1\). In particular, the electric-dipole operator \(\hat{\mathbf d}=q\hat{\mathbf r}\) is odd, so an E1 transition requires opposite initial/final parities. Such a parity condition is necessary, not sufficient: angular momentum and other selection rules may additionally restrict transitions.

Vanishing odd expectations, examples and degeneracy caveat

For any definite-parity state and parity-odd observable \(\hat O\), \(\avg O=-\avg O\), hence \(\avg O=0\). In particular \(\avg{\mathbf r}=\avg{\mathbf p}=0\) when these expectations exist. For the 1D harmonic oscillator \(V(x)=m\omega^2x^2/2\), \(\psi_n(-x)=(-1)^n\psi_n(x)\); for hydrogenic central-potential states, \(Y_l^m(-\hat{\mathbf r})=(-1)^lY_l^m(\hat{\mathbf r})\). Parity alone does not force energy degeneracy: the group \(\mathbb Z_2\) has one-dimensional irreducible representations, and the 1D oscillator is a concrete nondegenerate parity-invariant example.

Examination conclusion. Inversion symmetry is \(\PP^\dagger\hat H\PP=\hat H\) or \([\hat H,\PP]=0\). Since \(\PP^2=I\), its eigenvalues are \(\pm1\). Parity is conserved, permits even/odd symmetry-adapted eigenstates and imposes selection rules; degeneracy is not required by inversion symmetry alone.

B9Scalar and vector operators under rotations
Original question

Distinguish between a scalar operator and a vector operator on the basis of rotations.

Detailed worked solution

Rotation convention and scalar law

For proper spatial rotation \(R\in SO(3)\), states transform actively by \(\UU(R)=e^{-i\theta\mathbf n\cdot\hat{\mathbf J}/\hbar}\). A rotational scalar \(\hat A\) obeys

\[\boxed{\UU^\dagger(R)\hat A\UU(R)=\hat A.}\]

For an infinitesimal rotation about axis \(i\), \(\UU=I-id\theta\hat J_i/\hbar+O(d\theta^2)\), \(\UU^\dagger=I+id\theta\hat J_i/\hbar+O(d\theta^2)\), giving

\[\UU^\dagger\hat A\UU=\hat A+\frac{id\theta}{\hbar}[\hat J_i,\hat A]+O(d\theta^2).\]

Scalar invariance implies

\[\boxed{[\hat J_i,\hat A]=0\quad(i=x,y,z).} \label{eq:scalar}\]

Examples include \(\hat{\mathbf r}^{\,2}\), \(\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}\), \(\hat{\mathbf J}^{\,2}\), and \(\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}\) under rotations generated by total angular momentum.

Finite vector law and infinitesimal derivation

A Cartesian vector operator \(\hat{\mathbf V}=(\hat V_x,\hat V_y,\hat V_z)\) satisfies

\[\boxed{\UU^\dagger(R)\hat V_i\UU(R)=\sum_jR_{ij}\hat V_j.} \label{eq:vector-finite}\]

For a rotation by \(d\theta\) about \(z\), the geometric law yields

\[\hat V_x'=\hat V_x-d\theta\hat V_y,\quad \hat V_y'=\hat V_y+d\theta\hat V_x,\quad \hat V_z'=\hat V_z.\]

On the other hand, the unitary expansion gives \(\hat V_j'=\hat V_j+(id\theta/\hbar)[\hat J_z,\hat V_j]\). Comparing the two gives

\[\boxed{[\hat J_z,\hat V_x]=i\hbar\hat V_y,\quad [\hat J_z,\hat V_y]=-i\hbar\hat V_x,\quad[\hat J_z,\hat V_z]=0.}\]

For an infinitesimal rotation about arbitrary \(\mathbf n\), geometric variation is \(\delta\hat V_j=d\theta\sum_{i,k}\varepsilon_{jik}n_i\hat V_k\), while the unitary expansion is \(\delta\hat V_j=(id\theta/\hbar)\sum_i n_i[\hat J_i,\hat V_j]\). Since \(\mathbf n\) is arbitrary, and \(\varepsilon_{jik}=-\varepsilon_{ijk}\), the defining commutator follows:

\[\boxed{[\hat J_i,\hat V_j]=i\hbar\sum_k\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat V_k.} \label{eq:vector-comm}\]

Canonical verification with position and momentum

For a spinless particle, \(\hat L_i=\sum_{a,b}\varepsilon_{iab}\hat x_a\hat p_b\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}[\hat L_i,\hat x_j] &=\sum_{a,b}\varepsilon_{iab}\hat x_a[\hat p_b,\hat x_j] =-i\hbar\sum_a\varepsilon_{iaj}\hat x_a =i\hbar\sum_k\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat x_k,\\ [\hat L_i,\hat p_j] &=\sum_{a,b}\varepsilon_{iab}[\hat x_a,\hat p_j]\hat p_b =i\hbar\sum_k\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat p_k.\end{aligned}\]

Thus position and momentum are vector operators. For a scalar example,

\[[\hat L_i,\hat{\mathbf r}^{\,2}] =i\hbar\sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{ijk}(\hat x_k\hat x_j+\hat x_j\hat x_k)=0\]

because the parenthesized factor is symmetric in \(j,k\), while \(\varepsilon_{ijk}\) is antisymmetric.

Spin–orbit scalar and spherical tensors

Both \(\hat{\mathbf L}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf S}\) transform as vectors under total rotations generated by \(\hat{\mathbf J}=\hat{\mathbf L}+\hat{\mathbf S}\). Their dot product commutes with each \(\hat J_i\):

\[[\hat J_i,\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}] =i\hbar\sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{ijk}(\hat L_k\hat S_j+\hat L_j\hat S_k)=0.\]

A scalar is a rank-0 irreducible spherical tensor, while a vector is rank 1 with spherical components

\[T^{(1)}_{+1}=-\frac{V_x+iV_y}{\sqrt2},\qquad T^{(1)}_0=V_z,\qquad T^{(1)}_{-1}=\frac{V_x-iV_y}{\sqrt2}.\]

These obey \([J_z,T^{(1)}_q]=\hbar qT^{(1)}_q\) and \([J_\pm,T^{(1)}_q]=\hbar\sqrt{(1\mp q)(1\pm q+1)}\,T^{(1)}_{q\pm1}\). Under proper rotations, polar vectors (\(\mathbf r,\mathbf p\)) and axial vectors (\(\mathbf L,\mathbf S\)) obey the same rank-1 law; they differ under inversion.

B10Continuous rotational symmetry and its consequences
Original question

What is meant by continuous rotational symmetry of a Hamiltonian? What are its consequences?

Detailed worked solution

Full rotation invariance implies generator commutators

The Hamiltonian has full continuous rotational symmetry if

\[\boxed{\UU^\dagger(R)\hat H\UU(R)=\hat H\quad\text{for all }R\in SO(3),} \label{eq:rotinv}\]

where \(\UU(R)=\exp[-i\theta\mathbf n\cdot\hat{\mathbf J}/\hbar]\). For an infinitesimal rotation about the \(i\)-axis,

\[\begin{aligned}\UU_i(d\theta)&=I-\frac{id\theta}{\hbar}\hat J_i+O(d\theta^2),\\ \UU_i^\dagger(d\theta)&=I+\frac{id\theta}{\hbar}\hat J_i+O(d\theta^2).\end{aligned}\]

Insert into Eq. (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:rotinv):

\[\hat H+\frac{id\theta}{\hbar}(\hat J_i\hat H-\hat H\hat J_i)+O(d\theta^2)=\hat H.\]

For arbitrary \(d\theta\) this yields

\[\boxed{[\hat H,\hat J_x]=[\hat H,\hat J_y]=[\hat H,\hat J_z]=0.} \label{eq:rotcomm}\]

Conversely, if all generator commutators vanish, \(\hat H\) commutes with their exponentials, establishing finite rotational invariance under the usual common-domain assumptions.

Operator conservation and total angular momentum squared

The Heisenberg equation gives \(\dot{\hat J}_{i,H}=(i/\hbar)[\hat H_H,\hat J_{i,H}]=0\), so

\[\boxed{\frac{d\hat{\mathbf J}_H}{dt}=\mathbf0,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\avg{\hat{\mathbf J}}=\mathbf0.}\]

Also, with \(\hat{\mathbf J}^{\,2}=\sum_i\hat J_i^2\),

\[[\hat H,\hat{\mathbf J}^{\,2}] =\sum_i([\hat H,\hat J_i]\hat J_i+\hat J_i[\hat H,\hat J_i])=0.\]

Hence \(\hat{\mathbf J}^{\,2}\) is conserved. Independently the angular momentum algebra implies \([\hat{\mathbf J}^{\,2},\hat J_i]=0\). In particular \(\hat H,\hat{\mathbf J}^{\,2},\hat J_z\) commute pairwise and admit a common eigenbasis in each discrete-energy sector:

\[\begin{aligned}\hat H\ket{E,j,m,\alpha}&=E\ket{E,j,m,\alpha},\\ \hat{\mathbf J}^{\,2}\ket{E,j,m,\alpha}&=\hbar^2j(j+1)\ket{E,j,m,\alpha},\\ \hat J_z\ket{E,j,m,\alpha}&=\hbar m\ket{E,j,m,\alpha}. \label{eq:jstate}\end{aligned}\]

Here \(\alpha\) collects additional labels, and \(m=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j\). One cannot generally diagonalize \(J_x,J_y,J_z\) simultaneously because \([J_x,J_y]=i\hbar J_z\), despite their individual conservation.

Ladder algebra and rotational degeneracy

Define \(\hat J_\pm=\hat J_x\pm i\hat J_y\). From Eq. (see \(\LaTeX\) source: eq:rotcomm),

\[\boxed{[\hat H,\hat J_\pm]=0,\quad[\hat J_z,\hat J_\pm]=\pm\hbar\hat J_\pm, \quad[\hat{\mathbf J}^{\,2},\hat J_\pm]=0.}\]

If \(\hat H\ket{E,j,m}=E\ket{E,j,m}\), then

\[\hat H(\hat J_\pm\ket{E,j,m}) =\hat J_\pm\hat H\ket{E,j,m} =E(\hat J_\pm\ket{E,j,m}).\]

The state has the same energy but \(J_z\)-eigenvalue \(\hbar(m\pm1)\). Normalization follows from

\[\hat J_\mp\hat J_\pm=\hat{\mathbf J}^{\,2}-\hat J_z^2\mp\hbar\hat J_z,\]

so

\[\boxed{\hat J_\pm\ket{j,m} =\hbar\sqrt{(j\mp m)(j\pm m+1)}\ket{j,m\pm1}.} \label{eq:ladder}\]

The ladder stops at \(m=\pm j\), providing \(m=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j\), a total of

\[\boxed{g_j=2j+1} \label{eq:mult}\]

linearly independent states in a single irreducible \(j\)-multiplet. All have equal energy, since the ladder operators commute with \(H\). Different \(m\)-states are orthogonal as distinct eigenvectors of Hermitian \(J_z\).

Central potentials: direct commutator verification

For a spinless particle in a central potential \(\hat H_0=\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}/(2m)+V(r)\), total angular momentum reduces to orbital \(\hat{\mathbf L}\). The canonical relations give \([\hat L_i,\hat p_j]=i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat p_k\), whence

\[[\hat L_i,\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}] =i\hbar\sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{ijk}(\hat p_k\hat p_j+\hat p_j\hat p_k)=0.\]

Also, \([\hat p_b,V(r)]=-i\hbar\partial_b V\), so

\[\begin{aligned}[\hat L_i,V(r)] &=-i\hbar\sum_{a,b}\varepsilon_{iab}\hat x_a\partial_bV\\ &=-i\hbar\frac{V'(r)}r\sum_{a,b}\varepsilon_{iab}\hat x_a\hat x_b=0.\end{aligned}\]

Therefore \([\hat H_0,\hat L_i]=0\). Energy eigenfunctions can be chosen as \(R_{n_rl}(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)\) with \(E_{n_rlm}=E_{n_rl}\); a fixed \((n_r,l)\) multiplet has \(2l+1\) orbital states.

Spin–orbit coupling: only total angular momentum need be conserved

For a spinful particle with \(\hat{\mathbf J}=\hat{\mathbf L}+\hat{\mathbf S}\), consider

\[\hat H=\frac{\hat{\mathbf p}^{\,2}}{2m}+V(r)+\lambda(r)\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}.\]

The central terms are scalars. Since \([J_i,L_j]=i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}L_k\) and \([J_i,S_j]=i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}S_k\),

\[[J_i,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S]=i\hbar\sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{ijk}(L_kS_j+L_jS_k)=0\]

by antisymmetry. Therefore \([H,J_i]=0\). But

\[[L_z,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S]=i\hbar(L_yS_x-L_xS_y) =-[S_z,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S],\]

so orbital and spin angular momentum may exchange angular momentum while their sum is conserved. The angular eigenvalue

\[\boxed{\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S=\frac12(J^2-L^2-S^2) \longrightarrow\frac{\hbar^2}{2}[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-s(s+1)]}\]

shows that spin–orbit interactions may split levels by \(j\) but preserve the \((2j+1)\)-fold magnetic degeneracy for each fixed \(j\) multiplet. For nonconstant \(\lambda(r)\), the radial equation also changes; the angular eigenvalue alone is not a complete energy formula.

Scope and limitations

Full \(SO(3)\) invariance guarantees equal energies within a complete irreducible \(j\) representation; it does not require every energy eigenspace to have exactly \(2j+1\) states. Other symmetries can add accidental or dynamical degeneracies, and a \(j=0\) singlet is permitted. A uniform external magnetic field along \(z\), e.g. \(H_B=-\gamma B S_z\), preserves only axial rotations: \([H_B,S_z]=0\), but \([H_B,S_x]=-i\hbar\gamma B S_y\ne0\); the \(m\)-levels generally split. Spinor states carry representations of \(SU(2)\), the double cover of \(SO(3)\), allowing half-integer \(j\) while retaining the same multiplet dimension \(2j+1\).

Examination conclusion. Continuous rotational invariance gives \(U^\dagger H U=H\) for every rotation, hence \([H,J_i]=0\), conservation of \(\mathbf J\) and \(J^2\), simultaneous \(H,J^2,J_z\) eigenstates, and the symmetry-protected \((2j+1)\)-dimensional multiplet at fixed \(j\). Central potentials and spin–orbit Hamiltonians exhibit this structure; axial symmetry alone does not ensure the full degeneracy.
