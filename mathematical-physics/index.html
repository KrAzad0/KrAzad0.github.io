Mathematical Physics

Dimensions, geometry and differential equations: from 1D to 5D

Kumar Azad

M.Sc.-level learning notes · 10 November 2026

Abstract

What does the dimension of a mathematical-physics model actually mean? Starting with linear independence and coordinates, these notes build a unified formulation on Euclidean \(\mathbb R^n\), with detailed comparisons for \(n=1,2,3,4,5\). We derive gradients and radial Laplacians, compute hyperspherical volume elements, and extend classical and quantum equations to \(n\) dimensions. A particle in an \(n\)-dimensional box supplies a solved boundary-value problem. Spatial dimensions, spacetime dimensions, phase-space dimensions and physical units are carefully distinguished.

Status. Educational synthesis of standard mathematics and physics. Higher-dimensional Euclidean examples are mathematical models, not claims that our observed physical space has four or five independent spatial directions.

1. What is a dimension?

The dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space is the number of vectors in any basis. A basis is linearly independent and spans the entire space. In Euclidean space \(\mathbb R^n\), every point is specified by \(n\) real coordinates, and every vector can be written as a linear combination of \(n\) basis vectors:

\[ \mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n),\qquad \mathbf x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\mathbf e_i,\qquad \dim\mathbb R^n=n. \]

Independent coordinates are essential. For example, the condition \(x_3=x_1+x_2\) describes a plane inside \(\mathbb R^3\). Although points are written using three numbers, only two may be chosen freely, so the plane has intrinsic dimension two:

\[ (x_1,x_2,x_3)=s(1,0,1)+t(0,1,1), \qquad (s,t)\in\mathbb R^2. \]

Do not confuse this with the physical dimensions of units, such as length \([L]\), mass \([M]\) or time \([T]\). Those are a different concept called dimensional analysis. A two-dimensional surface in \(\mathbb R^3\) and a quantity with physical units of area \(L^2\) both involve the number two, but they describe different things. The definition via bases is treated in MIT's linear-algebra lectures.

2. Vector and tensor framework in \(\mathbb R^n\)

Take the standard orthonormal basis \(\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j=\delta_{ij}\). For vectors \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^n\), the Euclidean inner product and norm are

\[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\sum_{i=1}^n u_iv_i,\quad \|\mathbf x\|^2=\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2,\quad ds^2=\delta_{ij}\,dx^i dx^j. \]

The final expression uses Einstein summation: a repeated upper and lower index is summed over \(1,\ldots,n\). A linear transformation is represented by an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\), with \(y_i=A_{ij}x_j\). An orthogonal change of Cartesian axes preserves lengths:

\[ \mathbf y=Q\mathbf x,\qquad Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I_n, \qquad \|\mathbf y\|^2=\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}Q\mathbf x=\|\mathbf x\|^2. \]

In a general coordinate system \(q^i\), the line element can instead involve a position-dependent metric \(g_{ij}(q)\). The flat Cartesian case studied below has \(g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\). The derivative, metric and integration rules are then straightforward to generalize.

3. Gradient, divergence and Laplacian

Let \(f:\mathbb R^n\rightarrow\mathbb R\) be twice differentiable and \(\mathbf F:\mathbb R^n\rightarrow\mathbb R^n\) a differentiable vector field. The Cartesian differential operators are

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_nf&=\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf e_i\,\partial_i f,\\ \nabla_n\cdot\mathbf F&=\sum_{i=1}^n\partial_iF_i,\\ \Delta_nf&=\nabla_n\cdot\nabla_nf =\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i^2}. \end{aligned} \]

3.1 Derivation of the radial Laplacian

If \(f=f(r)\) depends only on the radius \(r=(x_1^2+\cdots+x_n^2)^{1/2}\), then, away from \(r=0\), the chain rule gives

\[ \partial_if=f'(r)\frac{x_i}{r},\qquad \partial_i^2f=f''(r)\frac{x_i^2}{r^2} +f'(r)\left(\frac1r-\frac{x_i^2}{r^3}\right). \]

Summing over \(i\) and using \(\sum_i x_i^2=r^2\) yields the central dimension-dependent identity:

\[ \boxed{ \Delta_n f(r) =f''(r)+\frac{n-1}{r}f'(r) =\frac{1}{r^{n-1}}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^{n-1}\frac{df}{dr}\right). } \]

It immediately supplies a check: for \(f(r)=r^2\), one has \(\Delta_n r^2=2n\). More generally, for \(r>0\),

\[ \Delta_n r^\alpha=\alpha(\alpha+n-2)\,r^{\alpha-2}. \]

The full hyperspherical decomposition also has an angular term \(\Delta_{S^{n-1}}\), which vanishes for a purely radial function:

\[ \Delta_n=\frac1{r^{n-1}}\partial_r \left(r^{n-1}\partial_r\right) +\frac1{r^2}\Delta_{S^{n-1}},\qquad n\geq2. \]

4. One through five dimensions: explicit formulations

For a radial function \(f(r)\), each additional coordinate increases the coefficient of \(f'(r)/r\) by one. The table summarizes the five cases. Here \(r=\|\mathbf x\|\), and all radial expressions are evaluated at \(r>0\).

Table 1. Euclidean spatial dimension, coordinates and radial Laplacian.
DimensionCoordinates\(\Delta_n f(r)\)
1D\(x_1\)\(f''(r)\)
2D\(x_1,x_2\)\(f''(r)+r^{-1}f'(r)\)
3D\(x_1,x_2,x_3\)\(f''(r)+2r^{-1}f'(r)\)
4D\(x_1,\ldots,x_4\)\(f''(r)+3r^{-1}f'(r)\)
5D\(x_1,\ldots,x_5\)\(f''(r)+4r^{-1}f'(r)\)

Expand the examples to see what a dimension changes in a simple differential equation \(\Delta_n f=0\) away from the origin.

1D — one independent coordinate

A point has the coordinate \(x\in\mathbb R\). The Laplace equation is an ordinary differential equation, and its general solution on an interval is affine.

\[ \frac{d^2f}{dx^2}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(x)=A+Bx. \]

With boundary values \(f(0)=0\) and \(f(L)=1\), the unique solution is \(f(x)=x/L\). The entire boundary of \((0,L)\) consists of two endpoints.

2D — two independent coordinates

The Cartesian equation \(\partial_x^2f+\partial_y^2f=0\) becomes \(f''(r)+f'(r)/r=0\) for radial functions. Multiply by \(r\) and integrate:

\[ (rf')'=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'(r)=\frac Br \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(r)=A+B\log(r/r_0). \]

The scale \(r_0>0\) makes the logarithm dimensionless. The logarithmic solution is valid on domains excluding \(r=0\), where it is singular.

3D — ordinary Euclidean physical space

For \(n=3\), the radial equation is \((r^2f')'=0\). Its solutions contain the familiar inverse-radius term:

\[ (r^2f')'=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(r)=A+\frac Br,\qquad r>0. \]

Inverse-radius behavior is characteristic of the Laplace equation in three spatial dimensions away from sources. To introduce a point source at the origin one needs a distributional equation.

4D — four Euclidean spatial coordinates

The coordinate is \(\mathbf x=(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)\). Four-dimensional Euclidean harmonic functions solve \(f''+3f'/r=0\), with radial solutions

\[ (r^3f')'=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(r)=A+\frac{B}{r^2},\qquad r>0. \]

This is an example of four spatial dimensions, not automatically the usual relativistic spacetime with three spatial coordinates plus time.

5D — five Euclidean spatial coordinates

With five independent spatial coordinates \(\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_5)\), the radial Laplacian is \(f''+4f'/r\). Consequently,

\[ (r^4f')'=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(r)=A+\frac{B}{r^3},\qquad r>0. \]

The stronger inverse-power behavior follows from the greater surface area growth \(S_4(r)\propto r^4\), not from introducing a fifth physical force or an observed extra dimension.

Unified result. For \(n\geq3\), radial harmonic functions away from the origin have the form \(A+B r^{2-n}\). At \(n=2\), the power-law exponent becomes zero and is replaced by a logarithm; at \(n=1\), solutions are linear on each connected interval.

5. Integration, hyperspheres and the Gamma function

In \(\mathbb R^n\), the volume measure is \(d^n x=dx_1\cdots dx_n\). A change to hyperspherical coordinates separates one radius from \(n-1\) angular variables:

\[ d^n x=r^{n-1}\,dr\,d\Omega_{n-1},\qquad \int_{\mathbb R^n}F(r)\,d^n x =S_{n-1}\int_0^\infty F(r)r^{n-1}\,dr. \]

For \(n\geq2\), \(d\Omega_{n-1}\) is the solid-angle measure on the unit sphere; the same radial integration formula remains valid for \(n=1\) if the zero-sphere is interpreted as two points, so \(S_0=2\). To derive the area constant, compute the Gaussian integral twice:

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb R^n}e^{-\|\mathbf x\|^2}\,d^nx &=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx\right)^n =\pi^{n/2},\\ &=S_{n-1}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r^{n-1}dr =\frac{S_{n-1}}{2}\Gamma(n/2). \end{aligned} \]

Equating the results, and integrating the radial measure from zero to \(R\), proves the standard volume and surface-area formulas:

\[ \boxed{S_{n-1}=\frac{2\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)},\qquad V_n(R)=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}R^n,\qquad \frac{dV_n}{dR}=S_{n-1}R^{n-1}.} \]
Table 2. Volume of a radius-\(R\) ball, where \(V_n\) is \(n\)-dimensional measure.
Dimension\(V_n(R)\)Interpretation
1D\(2R\)Length of an interval
2D\(\pi R^2\)Area of a disk
3D\(\frac{4\pi}{3}R^3\)Volume of a ball
4D\(\frac{\pi^2}{2}R^4\)Four-dimensional volume
5D\(\frac{8\pi^2}{15}R^5\)Five-dimensional volume

The formulas follow from \(\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)\) and \(\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt\pi\). NIST's Digital Library of Mathematical Functions tabulates the general formula. Do not interpret four- and five-dimensional volume as ordinary cubic meters; their dimensions are \(L^4\) and \(L^5\).

6. Classical and quantum dynamics in \(n\) dimensions

6.1 Classical Lagrangian formulation

For a particle of mass \(m\) moving in Euclidean \(\mathbb R^n\) under a potential \(V(\mathbf x,t)\), the Lagrangian is \(L=T-V\). Applying the Euler–Lagrange equation to each independent coordinate gives

\[ L=\frac m2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot x_i^2-V(\mathbf x,t), \qquad \frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x_i} -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_i}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\ddot x_i=-\partial_iV. \]

For \(n\) configuration coordinates, the associated classical phase space generically has \(2n\) coordinates: \((x_1,\ldots,x_n,p_1,\ldots,p_n)\). Phase-space dimension is not the same as spatial dimension.

6.2 Nonrelativistic Schrödinger formulation

The natural Hilbert space for a spinless particle in this model is \(L^2(\mathbb R^n,d^nx)\), or \(L^2(\Omega)\) for a domain \(\Omega\subseteq\mathbb R^n\). The position-space Schrödinger equation reads

\[ i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} =\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Delta_n+V(\mathbf x,t)\right]\psi, \qquad \int_{\Omega}|\psi(\mathbf x,t)|^2\,d^n x=1. \]

For a time-independent potential \(V\), separating \(\psi(\mathbf x,t)=\phi(\mathbf x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}\) gives the stationary equation

\[ \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_i^2+V(\mathbf x)\right]\phi(\mathbf x)=E\phi(\mathbf x). \]

The dimension \(n\) changes the differential operator, integration measure and the available degeneracies. It does not change the basic requirement that the Hamiltonian be self-adjoint on a suitable domain. For bounded spatial regions, boundary conditions such as Dirichlet or Neumann must be specified; different conditions lead to different spectra.

7. Worked derivation: a particle in a \(n\)-dimensional box

Consider a free spinless particle inside the hypercube \(\Omega=(0,L)^n\). Assume \(V=0\) inside, an impenetrable exterior, and Dirichlet conditions \(\psi=0\) whenever any coordinate equals \(0\) or \(L\).

Separating the stationary wavefunction as \(\phi(\mathbf x)=X_1(x_1)\cdots X_n(x_n)\) yields \(n\) ordinary differential equations, one for each Cartesian coordinate:

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{X_i''(x_i)}{X_i(x_i)}=E, \qquad X_i(0)=X_i(L)=0. \]

Because each term depends on a different coordinate, each must equal a separation constant. The boundary conditions select positive integers \(k_i=1,2,3,\ldots\), with \(X_i(x_i)\propto\sin(k_i\pi x_i/L)\). Normalizing each factor gives the exact orthonormal eigenfunctions and energies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \phi_{k_1,\ldots,k_n}(\mathbf x) &=\left(\frac2L\right)^{n/2}\prod_{i=1}^{n} \sin\left(\frac{k_i\pi x_i}{L}\right),\\ E_{k_1,\ldots,k_n} &=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_i^2. \end{aligned} \]

The ground state has \(k_1=\cdots=k_n=1\). Thus its energy scales linearly with the number of independent confinement directions:

\[ E_0^{(n)}=\frac{n\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}, \qquad n=1,2,3,4,5. \]
Table 3. Ground-state energy in units \(E_{\mathrm{1D}}=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)\).
Spatial dimensionGround quantum numbers\(E_0^{(n)}/E_{\mathrm{1D}}\)
1D\((1)\)1
2D\((1,1)\)2
3D\((1,1,1)\)3
4D\((1,1,1,1)\)4
5D\((1,1,1,1,1)\)5

This is a mathematical generalization of a separable quantum model, not experimental evidence for higher spatial dimensions. The normalization and scaling can be checked directly using \(\int_0^L\sin^2(k\pi x/L)\,dx=L/2\).

8. Four spatial dimensions versus four-dimensional spacetime

In the previous sections, \(n\) always counted independent Euclidean spatial coordinates, and the metric had all-positive signs. Relativity uses a different geometry. Ordinary special relativity has three spatial coordinates and one time coordinate, giving a four-dimensional Lorentzian spacetime:

\[ x^\mu=(ct,x,y,z),\qquad ds^2=-c^2dt^2+dx^2+dy^2+dz^2 \quad\text{(signature }-+++\text{)}. \]

By contrast, four-dimensional Euclidean space has four positive directions, for example \(ds^2=dx_1^2+\cdots+dx_4^2\). A five-dimensional model could refer to five spatial coordinates, or to a \(4+1\) spacetime with four space and one time dimension. The number alone does not specify the metric, physics or causal structure.

Another important distinction. A system of \(N\) particles in ordinary three-dimensional space has \(3N\) configuration coordinates, even though the physical space remains three-dimensional. In general, dimension always belongs to a specified mathematical space.

9. Exercises and references

Suggested problems. (i) Verify that the constrained set \(x_3=x_1+x_2\) is two-dimensional by constructing a basis. (ii) From the Cartesian Laplacian, show \(\Delta_nr^\alpha=\alpha(\alpha+n-2)r^{\alpha-2}\) for \(r>0\). (iii) Derive \(V_4(R)\) and \(V_5(R)\) from the Gamma function. (iv) Confirm the normalization of \(\phi_{k_1,\ldots,k_n}\) and calculate the first excited energy for the 2D square. (v) Explain why a \(2N\)-dimensional classical phase space does not imply \(2N\) spatial directions.
